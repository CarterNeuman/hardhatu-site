---
id: concept-termination-for-default
title: Termination for Default
tier: free
category: Contracts & Business
definition: >
  A contract clause allowing one party, usually the owner, to end the contract
  because the other party failed to perform, such as abandoning the work, missing the
  schedule, or failing to pay subcontractors, typically only after written notice and a
  defined cure period the defaulting party fails to use.
whyItMatters: >
  Unlike [[concept-termination-for-convenience|termination for convenience]],
  termination for default is a serious accusation with real financial consequences for
  the defaulting contractor: it can trigger a call on their performance bond, damage
  their reputation and future bonding capacity, and expose them to the owner's costs of
  hiring a replacement contractor to finish the work, often at a higher price.
example: >
  A general contractor falls months behind schedule and stops paying subcontractors,
  who begin filing mechanic's liens. After issuing a written notice to cure and the
  contractor fails to correct the problems within the contract's specified cure period,
  the owner terminates the contractor for default and calls on their performance bond
  to fund completion by another contractor.
careerAngle: >
  A [[career-contracts-administrator|Contracts Administrator]] has to build an
  airtight paper trail (notices, cure periods, documented failures) before a default
  termination, since a termination that doesn't survive a legal challenge can convert
  into a wrongful termination claim against the owner. A
  [[career-construction-claims-consultant|Construction Claims Consultant]] is often
  brought in specifically to analyze whether a default termination was properly
  supported by the facts and the contract's own notice requirements.
whatGoesWrong: >
  An owner terminates a contractor for default without first issuing the required
  written notice and cure period the contract specifies. A court or arbitrator later
  finds the termination was procedurally improper regardless of whether the
  contractor's performance was genuinely deficient, converting what should have been a
  straightforward default termination into a wrongful termination the owner now owes
  damages for.
