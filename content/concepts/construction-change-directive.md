id: concept-construction-change-directive
title: Construction Change Directive
tier: free
category: Project Process & Lifecycle
definition: >
  A written order from the owner, issued through the architect, directing the
  contractor to proceed immediately with a change in the work, a change in scope,
  materials, or method, before the cost or schedule impact has been negotiated and
  agreed to, used specifically when work can't wait for a full
  [[concept-change-order|change order]] to be worked out first.
whyItMatters: >
  Sometimes a change has to happen right now, an unexpected condition, a code issue,
  and waiting weeks to negotiate price before proceeding would stall the whole project.
  A construction change directive lets work continue immediately while the actual cost
  and time impact get settled afterward, instead of holding the job hostage to a
  negotiation.
example: >
  A structural issue is discovered that requires an immediate design change to keep the
  schedule from stalling. Rather than waiting for the contractor and owner to agree on a
  price first, the architect issues a construction change directive ordering the work to
  proceed, with the final cost to be determined once the scope is fully known.
careerAngle: >
  A [[career-project-manager|Project Manager]] has to track work performed under a
  construction change directive carefully, since the final price isn't settled yet and
  thorough cost documentation is what determines what the contractor actually gets
  paid. A [[career-contracts-administrator|Contracts Administrator]] makes sure a
  directive eventually converts into a properly executed change order once the number
  is agreed, rather than staying open indefinitely.
whatGoesWrong: >
  A contractor performs several weeks of work under a construction change directive
  without tracking labor and material costs in real time, assuming they'll reconstruct
  the number later. When it's time to negotiate the final price, the contractor has no
  detailed record to support their cost claim, and the owner successfully negotiates the
  price down simply because the contractor can't prove what it actually cost.
