howHiringWorks: >
  It's worth being direct about this category's 2026 market, because
  it's genuinely uneven. Junior adjuster job postings have fallen close
  to 50% since early 2024, roughly three times the decline seen across
  entry-level roles economy-wide, as insurers lean on AI to handle
  routine claims work that used to go to new hires. That part of the
  market really has gotten harder. But senior-level adjuster postings
  remain about 80% above 2017 levels, and the underlying reason demand
  exists at all keeps growing: the US averaged roughly 23 major
  disasters a year from 2020 to 2024, up from about 3 a year in the
  1980s, and federal projections still expect around 21,600 adjuster
  openings a year from attrition and disaster surges even as the
  occupation shrinks overall. In practice, that means the fastest, most
  reliable way into this field in 2026 isn't a generic entry-level
  application, it's getting licensed and positioning for independent or
  catastrophe (CAT) deployment work, where demand tracks disaster
  frequency directly rather than a shrinking pool of junior openings.
  The license gets you in the door, but IA firms keep a short list of
  adjusters they call first when a storm hits, and that list is built on
  relationships, not just licensing. Adjusters earn a spot on it by
  writing fair, defensible estimates under pressure and being
  straightforward to work with, both of which come from handling real
  claims alongside someone more experienced, not from a course. See
  [[resume-insurance-claims|the Insurance & Claims Resume Guide]], which shows exactly where your licensing and claims history
  need to sit for a reviewer to see them first.
checklist:
  - step: "Get licensed in your state first, before you apply anywhere"
    detail: "Most state adjuster licenses can be completed in 2 to 10 weeks depending on study pace, and an unlicensed application is filtered out immediately in this category no matter how strong the rest of your background is."
  - step: "Consider an all-lines license with broad reciprocity"
    detail: "A license like Texas's All-Lines license covers residential, commercial, auto, and more, and is recognized in most other states, which matters if you want to pursue CAT deployment work across state lines."
  - step: "Decide early: staff adjuster or independent/CAT adjuster"
    detail: "Staff roles are steadier but have tightened hardest at entry level. Independent and CAT adjusting has a rougher income curve but tracks real, growing disaster demand rather than a shrinking junior applicant pool."
  - step: "Get Xactimate certified before you apply"
    detail: "It's named directly in nearly every adjusting job posting, and having it already in hand removes a filter most other applicants haven't cleared yet."
  - step: "Build a relationship with independent adjusting (IA) firms before disaster season, not during it"
    detail: "IA firms staff up fast when a catastrophe hits, and the adjusters who get called first are the ones already in their system, not the ones applying the week of the storm."
  - step: "If you're claims-adjacent rather than a pure adjuster, get IICRC water damage certified"
    detail: "It opens restoration-estimating and insurance-restoration-estimator roles that sit right alongside traditional adjusting and draw on the same disaster-driven demand."
whereToLook:
  - "Your state Department of Insurance licensing portal, as the first stop before any job search"
  - "Independent adjusting (IA) firms and CAT staffing companies, which hire ahead of and during disaster season"
  - "Regional and national property & casualty carriers directly, for staff adjuster roles"
  - "The National Association of Public Insurance Adjusters (NAPIA), if you're pursuing public adjusting specifically"
  - "Insurance restoration and estimating firms, if you're coming in through the restoration side rather than traditional claims"
credentialsToHaveReady:

  - "Xactimate certification"
  - "IICRC water damage restoration certification, if pursuing restoration-adjacent estimating work"
  - "Any documented catastrophe (CAT) deployment history, even a single one"
commonMistakes:
  - "Treating this as a purely solo, credential-only career. The independent adjusters who stay busy every catastrophe season are the ones IA firms already trust from past deployments, not just the ones with the most licenses."
  - "Applying to staff adjuster roles with no license in hand yet, when licensing is the single fastest lever available and the first thing every posting screens for."
  - "Leaning on generic 'customer service' framing instead of naming real claims volume, type, and outcomes, which is what actually reads as credible here."
  - "Leaving licensing status ambiguous, or not naming which states you're covered or reciprocity-eligible in, forcing a reviewer to ask a question your resume should already answer."
  - "Expecting the same volume of entry-level staff hiring that existed a few years ago, instead of adjusting strategy toward licensing, CAT readiness, and independent adjusting where real demand still lives."
