howHiringWorks: >
  Hiring in the trades rarely starts with a resume posted online. There
  are two main ways in, and they work differently enough that it's worth
  deciding which one you're aiming for before you do anything else. A
  union apprenticeship is a structured, paid [[concept-apprenticeship-program|apprenticeship program]] run jointly by a
  union and its signatory contractors: you apply to a specific local,
  often test and interview for a spot, and if accepted you earn a wage
  from day one while training toward journey-level certification over
  several years. The open shop, or non-union, route means applying
  directly to contractors and subcontractors who hire independently,
  often through a staffing agency that places you on jobsites while you
  build a track record, with less formal structure and faster entry but
  no guaranteed training path. See [[concept-union-vs-open-shop|Union vs. Open Shop]] for how the two models
  actually differ. Which one is realistic depends heavily on your trade
  and your region: electrical, plumbing, and elevator work skew heavily
  union in a lot of markets, while general labor, concrete, and
  residential framing have a lot of open shop hiring. Either way, what
  actually gets you hired first is availability, reliability, and a
  couple of basic credentials, not a polished application. And the
  timing actually favors you: skilled trades employment is projected to
  grow faster than the job market overall through 2034, electricians
  alone by nearly 10 percent, and the large majority of contractors say
  they can't find enough qualified workers right now. A tight white
  collar hiring market gets most of the headlines, but the trades are
  one of the genuine bright spots in it. Nobody expects you to already
  know the trade on day one. Foremen and training coordinators are
  looking for people who show up on time, listen, and want to learn, and
  the helpers who get kept on and referred to the next job are almost
  always the ones who treated the crew like people worth getting along
  with, not just a paycheck. Once you've picked a path, see [[resume-field-trades|the Field & Trades Resume Guide]] for
  exactly what to put on a certifications-first resume or application
  packet, and what to leave off.
checklist:
  - step: "Decide union or open shop first"
    detail: "This one decision shapes everything else on this list, how you apply, what the pay and training path look like, and where you should actually be looking. See Union vs. Open Shop below before you do anything else."
  - step: "Get your OSHA 10 card"
    detail: "It's inexpensive, takes about a day online, and a lot of general contractors require it for jobsite access even where it isn't legally mandated. Not having one puts you a step behind anyone who already does."
  - step: "If going union, find your trade's local and its application window"
    detail: "Search your state or metro area's Building and Construction Trades Council for a directory of local unions, then check that specific local's apprenticeship application window directly. Many locals only accept applications once or twice a year, not on a rolling basis."
  - step: "If going open shop, identify contractors and staffing agencies directly"
    detail: "Make a list of 5 to 10 local contractors or subcontractors in your trade plus a construction-focused staffing agency, and apply, call, or walk in directly. A staffing agency can place you on a jobsite fast while you build a real work history."
  - step: "Line up your documents before you need them"
    detail: "A government-issued ID, proof you're eligible to work, and reliable transportation. Some union halls and larger contractors also run a background check or drug test as part of onboarding, worth knowing about upfront rather than being surprised."
  - step: "Follow up in person or by phone, not just online"
    detail: "A lot of entry-level trade hiring still happens through a phone call or a face-to-face conversation with a foreman or hiring manager, not a submitted application sitting in a queue."
whereToLook:
  - "Your state or metro area's Building and Construction Trades Council, which lists every union local in the region by trade"
  - "A specific union local's own apprenticeship page, for example IBEW for electrical, UBC for carpentry, the Operating Engineers for heavy equipment, or the Laborers for general labor"
  - "Associated Builders and Contractors (ABC) or a similar open shop trade association's local chapter job board"
  - "Construction-focused staffing agencies such as PeopleReady, Tradesmen International, or BTI, which place workers directly on jobsites"
  - "apprenticeship.gov's Apprenticeship Job Finder, a federal directory of registered apprenticeship programs by trade and location"
  - "A local NCCER-accredited training center or community college construction program, often the fastest route into a pre-apprenticeship track"
credentialsToHaveReady:
  - "An OSHA 10 Construction card"
  - "A valid driver's license. Many jobsites are outside reliable public transit, and showing up on your own is often assumed"
  - "Government-issued ID and whatever documents you'll need to prove you're eligible to work"
  - "An NCCER Core Curriculum certificate, if you've completed any pre-apprenticeship training"
commonMistakes:
  - "Thinking you need to already know the trade before you apply. Almost every entry point, apprenticeship or open shop, is built around training people who don't know it yet; a bad attitude on day one turns employers away far faster than inexperience does."
  - "Waiting to build a polished resume before applying anywhere. Most entry-level trade hiring happens through a phone call, a walk-in, or a staffing agency placement, not a job board application."
  - "Missing a union apprenticeship's application window. Many locals only accept applications once or twice a year on a fixed schedule, not on a rolling basis, so showing up a month late means waiting for the next cycle."
  - "Treating union and open shop as interchangeable. They have different application processes, pay structures, and training paths, and picking one without understanding the difference wastes real time."
  - "Skipping the OSHA 10 card because it isn't legally required everywhere. A lot of general contractors require it for site access regardless of what the law says, and not having one can get you turned away on day one."
firstStepToday: "Look up your trade's local union through your state's Building and Construction Trades Council, or if you're going open shop, find and call three local contractors or a construction staffing agency near you today."
