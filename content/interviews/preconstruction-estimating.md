questions:
  - question: "Walk me through how you'd approach a takeoff when the drawings are incomplete or ambiguous."
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Comfort with ambiguity, which is the daily reality of estimating;
      drawings are rarely fully resolved by the time a bid is due.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Describe a real process, such as flagging the gap, making a
      documented assumption, or pricing a contingency or qualification
      into the bid, rather than claiming you'd just wait for
      clarification, which isn't realistic under a bid deadline.
  - question: "Tell me about a bid you lost. What did you learn from it?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Honesty and reflection: everyone in estimating loses bids, and
      refusing to name a real one is a bigger red flag than the loss itself.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Pick a real example and be specific about what you'd actually do
      differently, rather than a generic "I learned to double-check
      everything."
  - question: "What estimating or takeoff software have you used?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Practical readiness with the tools that make up most of the daily
      workflow (Bluebeam, On-Screen Takeoff, and similar).
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Be specific about which tools and what you actually did in them. If
      you lack direct experience, be upfront and mention any adjacent
      software experience.
  - question: "How do you use earned value management to tell whether a project is really on track, not just on schedule?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      (Cost Engineer) Real technical fluency with cost-control methods, not
      just familiarity with the term.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Walk through a concrete example of catching a project that looked
      fine on the calendar but was actually trending over budget, and how
      you caught it.
  - question: "How do you decide which risks to price into a bid versus qualify out entirely?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      (Estimator) Judgment under commercial pressure: pricing in every
      risk makes a bid uncompetitive, but qualifying out too much creates
      exposure later.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Give a real example of a specific risk decision you made and the
      reasoning behind it, not just an abstract framework.
  - question: "Tell me about a constructability issue you caught during design that would have been expensive to fix later."
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      (Preconstruction Manager) The core value this role is supposed to
      add, catching problems before they're built, not after.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Use a specific, real example and be clear about the actual cost or
      schedule impact you prevented, since that's the concrete case for
      this role's value.
  - question: "How do you handle a variation or change order where the contractor and owner disagree on quantities?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      (Quantity Surveyor) Precision and negotiation under a disagreement
      that's ultimately about money, not just measurement technique.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Walk through how you'd verify the actual quantities independently and
      present that evidence in a way both sides can accept, rather than
      just picking a side.
  - question: "How do you set overall bid strategy, margin and contingency, on a job with real unknowns?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      (Senior Estimator) Strategic judgment beyond the mechanics of a
      takeoff; this role owns whether the company should submit the bid at
      all, not just what it should cost.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Talk through how you'd weigh competitive pressure against real risk
      exposure, and be honest about a bid strategy call that didn't work
      out the way you expected.

Interviews in this category often include a practical component that most construction interviews don't: a sample takeoff exercise, a review of how you'd structure a bid summary, or being asked to walk through pricing on a real past project. That's because estimating accuracy is directly testable in a way "tell me about yourself" isn't, so be ready to actually demonstrate your process rather than just describe it. Expect real questions about comfort with ambiguity, such as incomplete drawings or unclear scope, since that's the everyday reality of the job, not an edge case.

The people interviewing you here are usually the chief estimator or preconstruction director directly rather than HR, and they care most about your track record: how many bids you've run, what kinds of projects, and your familiarity with the specific estimating and takeoff software they use. A portfolio matters more here than in most office roles: if you have a redacted sample estimate or bid summary you can walk through, bring it or be ready to discuss it in detail.
