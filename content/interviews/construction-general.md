questions:
  - question: "Tell me about your experience with construction."
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Whether you actually know what you're walking into. This isn't just
      about résumé history; an interviewer wants to hear that you
      understand the pace, the physical demands, and the way decisions get
      made on a jobsite versus in another industry.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      If you're new to the industry, don't oversell direct experience you
      don't have. Instead, connect what you do know (a related trade, a
      DIY project, working with your hands, managing logistics elsewhere)
      to what the job actually requires, and be specific about what you've
      done to learn the basics before walking in, such as a course, a
      certification, or informational interviews with people already in the
      field.
  - question: "How do you handle a tight deadline or a schedule that suddenly changes?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Construction schedules move constantly because of weather, late deliveries,
      and inspection delays. They're checking whether you'll adapt calmly or
      get rattled when the plan changes on day one.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Use a real example, even from outside construction, where a plan
      changed on you and you adjusted without losing track of quality or
      safety. Avoid a vague "I'm flexible"; walk through what you
      actually did.
  - question: "Describe a time you had to work as part of a team with people you didn't choose."
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      A jobsite is a mix of trades, subcontractors, and companies who
      didn't pick each other; cooperation across that mix, not just
      within your own crew, is a daily requirement.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Focus on communication, not conflict. A good answer shows you can
      surface a disagreement early and get to a resolution without it
      becoming personal or slowing the job down.
  - question: "What do you know about jobsite safety, and why does it matter to you personally?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Whether safety is something you'll actually practice or just
      something you'll say you care about. Companies get evaluated (and
      insured) partly on safety record, so this isn't a throwaway question.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Mention anything concrete you already know, such as OSHA 10, PPE basics,
      or hazard reporting, and be honest that you're still learning the
      rest. Naming a specific safety habit you'll commit to (stopping work
      to ask when something looks wrong, rather than guessing) lands
      better than a general "safety first" line.
  - question: "Are you comfortable with early mornings, physical work, and being outdoors in all weather?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      This is a real logistics question, not just small talk; a lot of
      career-changers underestimate this shift, and turnover in the first
      few months is expensive for the employer.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Answer honestly. If you're coming from an office job, acknowledge
      the adjustment directly rather than brushing past it; it builds
      more trust than pretending it won't be different.
  - question: "Do you have any questions for us?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Whether you did any homework on the company and whether you're
      actually evaluating this as a real career move, not just taking the
      first thing offered.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Ask something specific: what a typical project looks like for this
      role, how they measure success in the first 90 days, or what
      training/certifications they support. Avoid asking only about pay
      and schedule this early.

Most construction interviews aren't trying to trip you up; they're trying to answer one question: will this person show up, work safely, and get along with a crew they didn't choose? The questions below cover what comes up across nearly every role, from an apprentice interview to an office position. If you're interviewing for one specific role, also check whether there's a role-specific guide for it.
