tagline: The questions you'll almost certainly get if you're moving into construction from a completely different field, and how to turn "no direct experience" into an asset instead of a liability.
questions:
  - question: "Why are you leaving your current industry for construction?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Whether this is a considered decision or a whim they'll have to
      re-hire for in three months. Hiring and training a new person is
      expensive, so they're screening for commitment as much as skill.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Give a real, specific reason, such as wanting hands-on work, better pay
      growth, job stability, or being tired of a desk, and pair it with
      evidence you've actually looked into what the shift involves (a
      class, a certification, talking to people in the trade). "I've
      always liked building things" without any follow-through reads as
      unconvincing.
  - question: "What transferable skills do you think you bring from your last job?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Whether you can honestly translate your background instead of
      either overstating unrelated experience or underselling yourself
      entirely.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Get specific rather than generic. "I managed a team of 12 and was
      responsible for a $2M budget" translates concretely to project
      coordination; "I'm a hard worker" doesn't tell them anything. Retail
      and food service often bring real customer-facing and
      multi-tasking-under-pressure experience, so name it plainly.
  - question: "This role usually goes to someone with a few years of hands-on experience. Why should we take a chance on you instead?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      This is often asked directly, and dodging it looks worse than
      answering it. They're testing whether you've thought this through or
      will get discouraged and quit once the learning curve gets hard.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Don't get defensive. Acknowledge the gap honestly, then point to
      what you've done to close it (certifications, informational
      interviews, entry-level or apprenticeship-track roles you're
      targeting on purpose) and what you bring that someone with only
      trade experience might not, such as a reliability record, a different kind
      of problem-solving, or whatever is true for you.
  - question: "How do you feel about starting at an entry-level position and rebuilding seniority?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Ego, basically. A career-changer who was senior somewhere else
      sometimes struggles taking direction from someone younger or
      earlier-career than them once they're the "new person" again.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Answer this one directly and without hedging; say plainly that
      you're fine starting where the role starts, and if it's true,
      mention that you're looking at this as a long-term path, not a
      title.
  - question: "What have you done to actually learn about this industry before applying?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Real interest versus a mass application to anything hiring. This is
      where preparation actually shows.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Have a real, specific answer ready, such as a certification you've earned,
      a construction terms and processes resource you've been studying,
      or people you've talked to who work in the field. Vague enthusiasm
      without any of this is the single most common way career-changers
      lose this question.

If you're coming into construction from another field entirely, expect the interview to spend real time on *why* and *how ready are you*, not just *can you do the job*. That's not a bad sign; it usually means they're taking the possibility seriously enough to actually probe it, rather than screening you out before you get in the room. The questions below are the ones that come up specifically because of the career change, on top of (not instead of) the general questions any construction interview covers.
