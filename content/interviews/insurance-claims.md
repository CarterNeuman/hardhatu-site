  - question: "What's your current state adjuster licensing status, and which states are you licensed or eligible for reciprocity in?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Whether you can legally do the job in the territory they need covered;
      this is often a hard screening question asked immediately, not a
      formality later in the process.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Know your exact status going in. If you're not yet licensed, say so
      plainly and describe your concrete plan and timeline to get there
      rather than being vague about it.
  - question: "Are you willing and able to travel on short notice for catastrophe (CAT) deployment?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      A real logistical commitment, not a formality: CAT deployment can
      mean weeks away from home on short notice, and this question is
      screening for whether that's actually workable for you.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Answer honestly. If your circumstances make extended travel
      difficult, say so directly rather than agreeing and then struggling
      with it once deployed; it costs the employer far more to find that
      out later.
  - question: "How do you handle a situation where a client or policyholder is upset or aggressive?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      Composure under a genuinely stressful interaction: you're often
      meeting people on one of the worst days they've had (their home or
      business just got damaged), and that shapes how they show up.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Use a real example and focus on how you stayed professional and
      moved the conversation toward a resolution, not just how you
      "de-escalated" in the abstract.
  - question: "Walk me through how you'd write a scope of loss for storm damage you're seeing for the first time."
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      (Insurance Adjuster) Whether you have a real, repeatable process for
      documenting damage thoroughly and accurately under time pressure.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Describe your actual inspection and documentation process, such as
      systematic room-by-room or area-by-area review, photos, and
      measurements, rather than a general "I'm thorough."
  - question: "How do you reconcile your estimate when it comes in noticeably different from the insurer's own adjuster's number?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      (Insurance Restoration Estimator) Negotiation and documentation
      skill: discrepancies between estimates are routine, and how you
      handle them affects whether the repair actually gets funded properly.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Walk through how you'd identify the actual source of the discrepancy
      (missed scope, different unit pricing) and defend your number with
      documentation rather than just splitting the difference.
  - question: "How do you find covered damage that an insurer's own adjuster might have missed?"
    whatTheyreAssessing: >
      (Public Adjuster) Thoroughness and policy knowledge: the entire
      value proposition of this role is finding what a less-motivated
      initial inspection didn't.
    strongAnswerTips: >
      Talk through your actual inspection process and how you cross-check
      it against the policy language itself, not just a visual walkthrough.

Interviews in this category are usually run by insurance companies, independent adjusting firms, or restoration contractors rather than general contractors, a meaningfully different hiring culture than the rest of construction. Licensing status is typically the very first substantive question, before background or experience, so know exactly where you stand (active license, reciprocity-eligible, or which exam you still need) before you walk in. If the role involves catastrophe (CAT) deployment, expect direct questions about your real willingness and ability to travel on short notice for extended stretches; this is treated as a genuine logistical filter, not small talk.

Because Insurance Adjusters and Public Adjusters do similar work from opposite sides of the same claim, be clear in your own head about which side you're interviewing for, and let that show in how you talk about the role; an interviewer for a public adjusting firm will notice if you keep describing the job from the insurer's perspective, and vice versa.
