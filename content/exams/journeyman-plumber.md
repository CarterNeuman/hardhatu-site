Becoming a Journeyman Plumber runs through a state licensing board, not a single national test, so the exact structure, hours, and fees vary by state. Texas, used here as a representative example, runs a two-tier path: roughly 4,000 hours (2 years) as a registered apprentice to reach Tradesman Plumber, then roughly 8,000 more hours (4 years) under a licensed plumber to reach Journeyman, a combined path of about 12,000 hours, notably longer than electrical licensing's single roughly 8,000-hour path to journeyman in the same state.

Texas's plumbing board, the Texas State Board of Plumbing Examiners (TSBPE), administers its own exams directly, unlike the electrical board's use of a third-party vendor. Exams are based on whichever model plumbing code a jurisdiction has actually adopted, the International Plumbing Code (IPC) or the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), which differ in real technical requirements like drainage, venting, and fixture provisions, not just naming. Confirming the correct code before studying matters more here than it does for electrical work, where the NEC functions close to a single national standard.

Beyond Journeyman, most states' progression continues to Master Plumber, generally requiring additional years of experience as a Journeyman first and often required to pull permits or own a plumbing business, not just to perform the work itself. A separate backflow-prevention certification is commonly required on top of any of these tiers for plumbers specifically working on potable water systems, protecting drinking water from contamination.

OSHA 10 or 30 sits alongside plumbing licensure as a separate, state-by-state requirement, mandatory on public construction projects in several states but not a nationwide legal requirement for plumbers generally, the same pattern this site covers for electrical licensing.

Texas's fees stay modest relative to the years of documented hours behind them: a $40 exam fee and a $40 annual renewal fee for Journeyman specifically, plus a required 6 hours of continuing education each year to stay in good standing. Improperly installed plumbing can contaminate drinking water or cause property-damaging leaks and sewage backups, which is the real reason behind the rigor, not bureaucracy for its own sake.
