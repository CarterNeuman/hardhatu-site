Licensing for property and casualty adjusters is genuinely state-by-state: roughly 15 states require no individual adjuster license at all, while most others do, and a small group (including California, New York, and Hawaii) offer limited or no reciprocity, requiring their own separate education and exam regardless of what license you already hold. The workaround most career adjusters use is the "designated home state" (DHS) system: Texas and Florida both let a non-resident claim either state as their home-state license, and over 30 other states accept that home-state license through reciprocity, letting one license cover most of the country instead of testing state by state.

Using Texas's widely-used All Lines adjuster exam as a representative example (exact numbers vary by state), the exam runs 150 questions over 150 minutes with a scaled passing score of 70 or higher. Regardless of which state's exam you're sitting, expect a mix of foundational property-and-casualty concepts, indemnity, insurable interest, negligence, replacement cost versus actual cash value, occurrence versus claims-made liability forms, and a state-specific section covering that state's own unfair claim settlement practices laws and required claim-response timeframes, usually the hardest part of the exam precisely because it can't be studied from a general textbook. An All Lines designation typically extends licensing beyond standard property and liability work to also cover workers' compensation and marine claims.

Using Texas again as a representative example, the realistic fixed cost of getting licensed runs around $140: roughly $49 for the Pearson VUE exam itself, about $41 for fingerprinting and a background check, and a $50 state application fee, with any exam-prep course an optional add-on since Texas doesn't mandate pre-licensing education. Texas also offers an exam-exemption pathway worth knowing about: completing a TDI-approved 40-hour prelicensing course with a monitored final exam (the same 150-question, 70%-passing structure as the standalone exam) can substitute for sitting the separate state exam, as long as the license application is filed promptly citing that course completion. Once licensed, keeping the license active requires real ongoing effort too, Texas requires 24 continuing education hours every two years, including at least 3 hours of ethics and at least 12 hours of classroom or classroom-equivalent instruction, not just self-paced online study.

Beyond standard licensing, it's worth understanding how states handle sudden claim surges after a major disaster. When a government official declares a state of emergency, that state's insurance commissioner can activate a temporary emergency (or catastrophe) adjuster license, typically valid for less than 180 days, that waives the usual exam, fingerprinting, and background-check requirements. That waiver is specifically for nonresident adjusters who are already licensed and in good standing elsewhere; a resident of the affected state still has to go through the standard licensing process regardless of the emergency. It's a system built to get enough qualified, already-vetted adjusters into a disaster area quickly, not a shortcut into the profession itself.
