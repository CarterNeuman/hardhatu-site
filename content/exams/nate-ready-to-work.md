Ready to Work is deliberately NATE's lowest-barrier credential, built for someone entering the HVACR field with little to no formal education or training yet, not a working technician brushing up on material. It's taken entirely online, no proctor required, from any computer including home, and costs around $60 (a retake runs about $45), with a free downloadable study guide covering fundamental job knowledge rather than advanced technical material.

Passing it earns a certificate, specifically not a full NATE certification, a distinction NATE itself draws carefully. It's valid for 5 years before needing to be retaken to renew. The next step up, for someone with roughly 6 months to a year of real field experience, is the HVAC Support Technician Certificate, which does require a proctored exam at a Testing Organization and covers the same professional-level topics at a more foundational level, a deliberate bridge between Ready to Work and the real thing.

What people usually mean when they say a technician is "NATE certified" is the traditional path: a 50-question Core exam (70%, 35 correct, to pass) plus a specialty exam in Installation, Service, or at the Senior level, with NATE suggesting roughly a year of experience before attempting Installation, two years for Service, and five years before Senior level. That full certification lasts 2 years before renewal (3 years specifically for Ground Source Heat Pump Installer certification), maintained through continuing education hours rather than a full retake.

None of this replaces what's actually legally required. An EPA Section 608 certification is mandatory before anyone can legally work with regulated refrigerants, and most states require a Journeyman HVAC license before working unsupervised, both separate from and required regardless of any NATE credential, Ready to Work included. NATE certifications, at every level, are voluntary industry credentials layered on top of those legal requirements, not a substitute for them.

Worth knowing if you're using this site to plan a path into HVAC: Ready to Work isn't required to apply for an entry-level HVAC job or to start an apprenticeship. It exists to let someone demonstrate basic job-readiness before or very early in that process, which is exactly the gap it's built to fill.
