Every major provider, American Red Cross, American Heart Association, and others, teaches the same core material in slightly different packaging: recognizing a cardiac or breathing emergency, hands-only or full CPR with rescue breaths, using an AED, controlling severe bleeding, treating shock and burns, and handling choking, plus when to avoid moving someone with a possible spinal injury. A blended or in-person course pairs a short knowledge check with a real hands-on skills session, practicing compressions on a manikin, placing AED pads correctly, while a purely self-paced online course skips that hands-on check entirely.

That hands-on piece matters for more than just learning the material well. OSHA's construction standard, 29 CFR 1926.50, requires a person trained in first aid to be available whenever there's no infirmary, clinic, hospital, or physician that's reasonably accessible in terms of time and distance to a jobsite, no fixed mileage or minute number, just a judgment call based on how remote or urban the site actually is. The standard names the American Red Cross and the U.S. Bureau of Mines directly as acceptable training sources but also allows equivalent training that can be documented, which is why American Heart Association Heartsaver courses, not named directly in the regulation's text, are just as widely accepted by employers in practice.

This is also where course format actually affects compliance, not just convenience. Red Cross itself flags that a fully online certificate may not satisfy an employer's OSHA-driven workplace requirement the way a blended or in-person course does, since online-only skips the verified hands-on skills demonstration an employer may be relying on. If the goal is meeting a real jobsite requirement rather than just building personal knowledge, it's worth confirming with the specific employer which format they'll actually accept before paying for the cheaper online-only option.

Cost runs roughly $60 to $100 for a combined CPR, AED, and First Aid course, varying by provider, location, and exactly which modules get bundled together (a CPR-only course without the First Aid content typically costs less). Certification holds for 2 years before it needs renewing, and OSHA's own first aid kit requirements travel alongside the training requirement: supplies kept in a weatherproof container with individually sealed packages for each item, checked before being sent to a site and inspected at least weekly afterward.

None of this is complicated material to study, but it's worth actually practicing rather than just reading about. Compression rate and depth, AED pad placement, and the difference between direct pressure and a tourniquet for severe bleeding are all things that are easy to describe and much harder to do correctly under real pressure, which is exactly why the hands-on skills session exists in the first place.
