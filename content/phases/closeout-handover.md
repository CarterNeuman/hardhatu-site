title: Closeout & Handover
tier: free
order: 7
whatHappens: >
  Closeout is the phase that turns a finished building into a building someone is
  actually allowed to use, and it's rarely as fast as it looks from the outside. Getting
  the last piece of trim installed and getting the project formally, legally closed out
  are two different milestones, and the second one can take weeks or months after the
  first.

  It starts with the jurisdiction's own final inspections and any required
  [[concept-special-inspection|special inspections]] getting finalized and filed, which
  clear the way for the local building department to issue a
  [[concept-certificate-of-occupancy|certificate of occupancy]] (or a temporary one),
  the actual legal permission to occupy the space, closing the loop that opened back in
  Permitting & Approvals. That milestone usually lines up closely with
  [[concept-substantial-completion|substantial completion]]: the point where the owner
  can actually use the building for its intended purpose, even if small items remain.
  Occupancy is not the finish line, though. Substantial completion triggers a formal
  [[concept-punch-list|punch list]] walkthrough, the architect, owner, and contractor
  tour the finished building together and document every remaining deficiency, a
  scuffed wall, a door that doesn't latch right, a light fixture that's the wrong color
  temperature, which the contractor then has a defined window to fix. In parallel,
  [[concept-commissioning|commissioning]] verifies that building systems, HVAC, fire
  alarm, elevators, don't just exist but actually operate as designed. Only once the
  punch list and commissioning both clear is the project considered fully, finally
  complete, often weeks or months after people have already moved in.

  Behind the physical walkthrough, a parallel paperwork closeout has to finish too:
  [[concept-as-built-drawings|as-built drawings]] get compiled, showing what was actually
  built rather than just what was originally designed, since the two rarely match
  exactly by the end. [[concept-warranty|Warranty]] documentation gets assembled and
  handed over, [[concept-lien-waiver|lien waivers]] get collected from every
  subcontractor confirming they've been paid and won't file a claim against the
  property, and [[concept-retainage|retainage]], the percentage withheld from every
  payment throughout Construction specifically to protect the owner until this moment,
  finally gets released. [[concept-turnover|Turnover]] itself, handing over keys,
  as-builts, warranties, and system manuals to whoever will actually run the building day
  to day, is the last formal step before the project stops being a construction project
  and starts being a building.
timeline: >
  Substantial completion to final closeout typically takes four to eight weeks on a
  mid-size commercial project, longer on anything with complex building systems that
  need extensive commissioning, or shorter on a simple project with a short punch list.
  What stretches it: a long or disputed punch list, a commissioning process that
  uncovers a system not performing as designed, or a jurisdiction slow to schedule its
  final inspections. What compresses it: a project that stayed on top of documentation
  throughout Construction instead of leaving it all for the end, and a punch list that's
  short because quality control caught most issues before this phase started.
costOfChangeCurve: >
  Closeout isn't really a phase where big changes still happen, most of the expensive
  decisions are long since made, but it's where every unresolved shortcut from earlier
  phases finally comes due. A system that was never properly commissioned, a permit
  condition that was never actually satisfied, or documentation that was never kept
  current doesn't just disappear; it surfaces here as withheld retainage that won't
  release, a certificate of occupancy that gets delayed, or a facilities manager handed
  documentation that doesn't actually match the building they're inheriting.
whoIsInvolved:
  - Quality Control Manager
  - Owner's Representative
  - Project Manager
  - Facilities Manager
commonMisconception: >
  People often assume that once the owner has moved in, the project is finished. In
  reality, occupancy and full project completion are two different milestones: a
  building can be legally occupied while its punch list and commissioning are still
  being finalized, sometimes for months afterward, and none of that remaining work can
  be meaningfully rushed without risking a system that fails once people are actually
  relying on it every day.
whatCanGoWrong: >
  A punch list that keeps growing instead of shrinking is the most common problem, often
  because the contractor and owner disagree about what actually counts as a deficiency
  versus an acceptable tolerance, which can drag closeout out for months if it isn't
  managed firmly. A close second is commissioning turning up a system that's installed
  correctly but never properly programmed, HVAC controls sequenced wrong so a system
  fights itself instead of running efficiently, which can force real troubleshooting at
  the worst possible time, after everyone has mentally moved on from Construction.

  Missing or incomplete documentation causes quieter damage: as-built drawings that were
  never actually updated as changes happened in the field leave the facilities manager
  running the building off inaccurate information for years afterward, and a lien waiver
  never properly collected from one subcontractor can leave an owner exposed to a claim
  long after they assumed the project was behind them.
nonLinearReality: >
  Closeout doesn't always wait for every last item to finish before the building starts
  its actual life. An owner can take a phased or partial certificate of occupancy for one
  finished wing of a building while punch list work continues in another, or move
  furniture and staff in during the exact weeks commissioning is still being finalized.
  The clean handoff this section describes, substantial completion, then punch list,
  then occupancy, is often several of those things happening on overlapping tracks
  rather than one after another.
