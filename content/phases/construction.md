title: Construction
tier: free
order: 6
whatHappens: >
  This is the phase most people picture when they think "construction": the building
  actually gets built. But it isn't one continuous activity; it's a sequence of trades
  handing a project off to each other in a specific order, where starting something too
  early usually costs more than starting it late.

  Take a typical mid-size commercial building, picking up on the graded, staked site
  that Site Preparation & Mobilization already handed over. Concrete crews inspect and
  pour footings and foundation walls first, with rebar and formwork checked before the
  pour rather than after, and framing on top only starts once the concrete has gained
  enough strength to bear that load, not once it's fully cured, which takes far longer.
  Structural framing follows, steel or wood, depending on the building, giving the
  project its actual shape for the first time. Only once the structure is enclosed (a
  "dried-in" building, meaning the roof and exterior walls are up) do the interior
  trades start, and they go in a specific order too: HVAC ductwork first, since it needs
  the most space and is the least flexible, then plumbing, which needs a working slope,
  then electrical last, since wiring is the easiest of the three to route around
  whatever's already there. Insulation and a rough-in inspection follow before drywall
  closes the walls up. Finishes, flooring, paint, fixtures, trim, come last, because
  they're the easiest thing to damage if earlier trades are still moving material
  through the space.

  Running beneath that visible sequence, a second process never stops: the paperwork
  that keeps the sequence honest. RFIs get filed and answered when drawings don't match
  reality. Change orders get signed whenever the scope, price, or schedule actually
  changes, whether the owner asked for it or a site condition forced it. Daily reports
  track what actually happened on site, in case a dispute needs reconstructing later. A project
  manager's real job during this phase isn't swinging a hammer; it's making sure the
  physical sequence and the paper trail stay in sync, so nobody's building something that
  was never actually approved.
timeline: >
  For a mid-size commercial building, Construction is usually the single longest phase on
  the calendar, typically twelve to twenty-four months, though a single-family home might
  see four to eight months of actual construction and a major hospital or high-rise can
  run past three years. What stretches it: weather delays during foundation pours and
  steel erection, material lead times arriving later than the schedule assumed, tight labor
  availability, and inspections that have to happen in sequence rather than all at once.
  What compresses it: prefabricating components off-site while the foundation is still
  curing, and a general contractor who starts early trade packages before every drawing
  is fully finished.
costOfChangeCurve: >
  By the time a project reaches Construction, the cost-of-change curve is already near
  its steepest point. A decision changed during Design might mean redrawing a wall on a
  computer; the same decision changed during Construction can mean tearing out concrete
  that already cured, or pulling wire that's already run through a wall that's already
  closed up. That's not because more mistakes happen on-site, it's because every phase
  before this one already assumed things would go a certain way, and undoing that
  assumption now means redoing physical work instead of a drawing. It's part of why a
  good [[concept-rfi|RFI]] process matters so much here: catching a conflict on paper
  before it's built is dramatically cheaper than catching it after.

commonMisconception: >
  A common misconception is that once permits are pulled and the contract is signed, the
  design is finished and Construction just executes it. In reality, a meaningful share of
  a project's RFIs get generated during Construction itself, meaning design and
  construction overlap more than outsiders expect: the design isn't fully finished, it's
  being finished in real time as crews hit details the drawings didn't fully resolve.
whatCanGoWrong: >
  Most construction-phase problems trace back to something happening out of order, or a
  decision getting made without being written down. A common one: a subcontractor gets
  delayed by a supply shortage on a specific material, but the crew scheduled to work
  right after them starts on time anyway, because nobody updated the schedule. Now two
  trades are trying to occupy the same space, and the second crew either stands around
  getting paid to wait, or works around the first crew badly enough that something needs
  to be redone later.

  Weather causes a similar chain reaction on foundation pours and steel erection
  specifically, since concrete needs the temperature within a certain range to cure
  properly and steel crews can't safely work high up in heavy wind: a week of bad
  weather during a pour doesn't just cost that week, it pushes every trade scheduled
  after it. And unclear drawings cause slowdowns that are easy to
  underestimate: a framing crew that hits a detail the plans don't fully explain either
  guesses (risky) or stops and waits for an answer (slow), and an RFI that takes a week to
  come back can stall a whole crew for that week if there's nothing else for them to work
  on. None of these are unusual or rare; they're the normal texture of a construction
  schedule, which is exactly why tracking RFIs and change orders closely, and building
  some slack into a schedule for weather, matters as much as it does.
nonLinearReality: >
  Construction itself rarely runs as one clean pass through the sequence this section
  describes. A change discovered on one floor can send a crew back to redo work on a
  floor that was supposedly already finished, punch-list-style fixes happen throughout
  rather than only at the very end, and under fast-track or design-build delivery, crews
  are often actively building one part of the structure while Design is still finishing
  the drawings for another part entirely, so "Design ends, then Construction begins"
  understates how much the two genuinely overlap on a real jobsite.
