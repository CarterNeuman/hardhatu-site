title: Site Preparation & Mobilization
tier: free
order: 5
whatHappens: >
  Before anything gets built, the site itself has to be made ready to build on, and the
  contractor has to actually set up shop. This phase is the unglamorous stretch between
  a signed contract and the first concrete pour, easy to mentally fold into
  "Construction" even though almost none of the finished building takes shape during it.

  [[concept-mobilization|Mobilization]] comes first: the general contractor moves in
  [[concept-temporary-facilities|temporary facilities]] (a site office trailer, portable
  toilets, temporary power and water), sets up fencing and site security, and puts a
  [[concept-site-logistics-plan|site logistics plan]] in place covering where deliveries
  come in, where material gets staged, and where workers park, so the site can actually
  function once dozens of trades start showing up. If anything existing has to come
  down, [[concept-demolition|demolition]] happens next, under the permit obtained back
  in Permitting & Approvals, clearing the site down to bare ground. Only then does a
  surveyor stake out the building's exact location and reference elevations from the
  earlier [[concept-site-survey|site survey]], since every trade after this one builds
  relative to those marks, followed by [[concept-excavation|excavation]] and
  [[concept-grading|grading]] to bring the site to the elevations the foundation design
  calls for.

  Running in parallel with the physical work, [[concept-erosion-control|erosion control]] measures
  (silt fencing, sediment basins) go in before any soil gets disturbed, since a
  stormwater permit obtained during Permitting usually requires them in place first, not
  as an afterthought. A site-specific [[concept-safety-orientation|safety orientation]]
  gets built for the specific hazards this site presents, heavy equipment traffic,
  excavation depth, whatever's unique here, and every worker who steps on-site goes
  through it before they're allowed to work. Utility connections
  ([[concept-site-utilities|site utilities]], new water, sewer, or power service) often
  get roughed in during this window too, since they're easiest to run before anything is
  built on top of them.
timeline: >
  For a small project, site preparation and mobilization might take one to three weeks.
  For a larger site with demolition, contaminated soil, or difficult utility work, it can
  stretch to two or three months before a single foundation form goes up. What stretches
  it: unexpected subsurface conditions (rock where the geotechnical report didn't predict
  it, contamination beyond what testing found), and utility relocations that depend on a
  utility company's own schedule, not the contractor's. What compresses it: a site that's
  already been used for a similar purpose (utilities and access already exist), and
  mobilization work that starts the moment the permit clears instead of waiting.
costOfChangeCurve: >
  Site Preparation is where a mistake starts costing real money to physically undo
  rather than just time to redraw. Grading the site to the wrong elevation, or staking
  the building in slightly the wrong location, is still fixable here, machinery can
  regrade, a stake can be reset, but it's earthwork and labor, not a drawing revision.
  Once concrete is poured on top of a bad elevation during Construction, the same mistake
  becomes demolition. This phase is the last point where the ground itself is still this
  forgiving.
whoIsInvolved:
  - Equipment Operator
  - Laborer
  - Safety Manager
  - Environmental/Remediation Specialist
commonMisconception: >
  People often think "construction starts" the day heavy equipment first shows up on
  site, assuming that's the excavator beginning the actual building. In reality, most of
  what happens in the first few weeks on a real jobsite, fencing, trailers, utility
  locates, erosion control, safety orientation, exists to make the site usable at all,
  and has nothing to do with the building itself yet.
whatCanGoWrong: >
  Unexpected subsurface conditions cause the most disruptive problems here: rock that a
  geotechnical report didn't fully capture, contaminated soil beyond what earlier testing
  found, or groundwater higher than expected, any of which can halt excavation and force
  a design change before work continues. A close second is skipping or rushing erosion
  control, since a storm hitting an unprotected site can wash sediment into a storm
  drain or a neighboring property, triggering an environmental violation and a stop-work
  order well before the building itself is even at issue.

  Utility conflicts are a quieter version of the same problem: a buried line that wasn't
  marked accurately, or a utility company that takes longer to relocate a line than the
  schedule assumed, can stall grading and excavation for weeks while everyone waits on a
  third party who isn't on the contractor's payroll and doesn't answer to the project
  schedule.
nonLinearReality: >
  Site Preparation doesn't always fully wrap up before Construction begins either. Deep
  utility connections, final grading around the building's edges, and landscaping prep
  are often left unfinished on purpose, sometimes deliberately sequenced after the
  building is framed and dried in, since heavy equipment driving across finished grading
  just damages it. "Site work finishes, then the building goes up" describes the bulk of
  it, but a meaningful piece of this phase's work routinely finishes alongside
  Construction rather than strictly before it.
