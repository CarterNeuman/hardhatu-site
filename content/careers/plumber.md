whatIs: >
  A Plumber installs and maintains the water supply, drainage, and gas
  piping systems in a building, everything from the rough-in piping run
  through framed walls before drywall goes up, to the sinks, toilets, and
  water heaters installed at the very end of a project. Like electrical
  work, plumbing has to pass inspection against a plumbing code before it
  can be covered up, and most commercial plumbers work for a plumbing
  subcontractor rather than the general contractor directly.
whatTheyDo:
  - Run water supply and drain-waste-vent (DWV) piping through framed walls and floors before drywall (rough-in)
  - Install fixtures, sinks, toilets, water heaters, backflow preventers, during trim-out
  - Read plumbing drawings and isometric diagrams to plan pipe routing before cutting anything
  - Pressure-test and inspect piping systems before they're covered up or put into service
  - Troubleshoot leaks, clogs, and pressure problems and trace them back to the source
  - Coordinate with electricians and HVAC crews so nobody's rough-in fights for the same wall or ceiling space
typicalDay:
  - time: "6:30 AM"
    activity: Review the day's task list and pull fittings and pipe for the rough-in scheduled that day
  - time: "7:00 AM"
    activity: Run DWV piping on the second floor, following the plumbing drawings' fixture locations
  - time: "10:00 AM"
    activity: Pressure-test yesterday's completed piping run before it gets covered
  - time: "1:00 PM"
    activity: Set fixtures in a unit that's ready for trim-out
  - time: "3:30 PM"
    activity: Walk finished rough-in with the inspector before the wall gets closed up
whereTheyWork:

skills:
  - Reading plumbing drawings and code
  - Pipe fitting and installation (copper, PVC, PEX)
  - Troubleshooting leaks and pressure issues
  - Blueprint math (pipe sizing, slope calculations)
  - Working safely with pressurized systems and occasionally gas lines

education:
  required: >
    High school diploma or equivalent, then a registered apprenticeship
    (typically 4-5 years of paid on-the-job training plus classroom
    hours); a Journeyman Plumber license is legally required by most
    states/municipalities before working unsupervised; an apprentice
    works under a licensed plumber until then
  helpful: >
    OSHA 10 or 30 certification (not a nationwide legal requirement,
    though several states, including New York, Massachusetts,
    Connecticut, Nevada, Missouri, and Rhode Island, legally require it
    for workers on public construction projects) and backflow-prevention
    certification for working on potable water systems
  notNecessary: A four-year college degree isn't part of this path; like electrical work, the apprenticeship-to-license route is standard and pays during training rather than costing tuition
