title: Surveyor
category: "Technology & Design"
tagline: Establishes the exact boundaries, elevations, and reference points a project gets built against, the one role where being off by an inch can mean a building in the wrong place.
tier: free
whatIs: >
  A (Land) Surveyor measures and maps land boundaries, elevations, and
  physical features, then translates that into the control points and
  benchmarks a construction crew builds from: where the building corner
  actually sits, what elevation the finished floor needs to be. Practicing
  as a Licensed Professional Land Surveyor requires state licensure, which
  in nearly every U.S. state means passing the NCEES Fundamentals of
  Surveying (FS) exam early in a surveying career and the NCEES Principles
  and Practice of Surveying (PS) exam after gaining qualifying experience,
  the same two-exam structure a licensed engineer goes through, run by the
  same national organization (NCEES).
whatTheyDo:
  - Establish property boundaries through legal research and field measurement
  - Set control points and benchmarks that a project's layout is built from
  - Perform topographic surveys documenting existing site conditions before design
  - Verify as-built conditions against design drawings during and after construction
  - Prepare legal descriptions and plats used in property records and permitting
  - Use GPS, robotic total stations, and increasingly drone-based scanning to collect field data
typicalDay:
  - time: "6:30 AM"
    activity: Load survey equipment and review the day's control points to establish or verify
  - time: "7:30 AM"
    activity: Set benchmarks and control points for a new building layout
  - time: "10:30 AM"
    activity: Perform a topographic survey of a site being considered for a future project
  - time: "1:00 PM"
    activity: Process the morning's field data in the office
  - time: "3:00 PM"
    activity: Verify as-built foundation locations against the design drawings before the next phase starts
whereTheyWork:
  - Jobsite and undeveloped land
  - Office (for data processing, legal research, and plat preparation)
skills:
  - Precise field measurement and equipment operation (GPS, total stations)
  - Reading legal property descriptions and site plans
  - Geometry and trigonometry
  - Attention to detail (small errors compound into real legal and construction problems)
  - Increasingly, drone and 3D scanning technology
software:
  - Surveying and mapping software (e.g. AutoCAD Civil 3D)
education:
  required: >
    Varies by state, but licensure as a Professional Land Surveyor (PLS)
    generally requires a surveying-related degree or qualifying
    experience, passing the NCEES Fundamentals of Surveying (FS) exam,
    several years of supervised experience, and then the NCEES Principles
    and Practice of Surveying (PS) exam
  preferred: Bachelor's degree in surveying or geomatics
  helpful: Drone/UAV pilot certification, as aerial data collection becomes more common in the field
  notNecessary: Entry-level field crew positions (survey technician/party chief track) don't require a degree; many surveyors start there and pursue licensure over time
progression:
  - Survey Technician
  - Party Chief
  - Licensed Professional Land Surveyor
  - Survey Manager or Owner of a Surveying Firm
hirerTypes:

  - Civil Engineering Firms
  - General Contractors (larger firms with in-house survey crews)
