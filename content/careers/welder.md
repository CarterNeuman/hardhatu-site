title: Welder
category: "Field & Trades"
tagline: Joins metal components with heat and pressure, and for structural work, has to prove it on a certification test before being allowed to do it on a real building.
tier: free
whatIs: >
  A Welder joins metal components using heat, pressure, or both: most
  visibly, the structural steel connections that hold a building's frame
  together, but also metal decking, handrails, and miscellaneous steel
  throughout a project. For structural steel work governed by the American
  Welding Society's D1.1 Structural Welding Code (the code most commercial
  structural steel is built to), a welder has to pass a certification test
  for the specific process, material, and position they'll actually be
  welding in before they're allowed to perform that code work: a real,
  contractually enforced requirement, even though it isn't a
  government-issued license the way a journeyman electrician license is.
whatTheyDo:
  - Weld structural steel connections according to approved shop drawings
  - Read welding symbols on drawings to determine the correct weld type, size, and location
  - Pass (and periodically re-pass) certification tests for the specific process and position required by the job
  - Inspect completed welds visually and prepare them for a certified welding inspector's review
  - Set up and maintain welding equipment, including selecting the correct filler metal for the base material
  - Work safely around fire hazards, fumes, and equipment operating at very high temperatures
typicalDay:
  - time: "6:30 AM"
    activity: Review welding symbols on the day's drawings and set up equipment for the required process
  - time: "7:00 AM"
    activity: Weld structural steel connections on the level currently being erected
  - time: "10:30 AM"
    activity: Stop for a certified welding inspector to review completed welds before the next connection is covered
  - time: "1:00 PM"
    activity: Re-certify on a welding process the current scope of work requires but hasn't used recently
  - time: "3:30 PM"
    activity: Clean up and inspect equipment before the next day's work
whereTheyWork:
  - Jobsite
  - Occasionally a shop for prefabricating steel assemblies
skills:
  - Reading welding symbols and structural drawings
  - Precision welding across multiple processes (e.g. stick, MIG, TIG) and positions
  - Visual weld inspection
  - Working safely with high heat and fire hazards
  - Physical stamina and steady hands in awkward positions
software:
  - none typically required for the trade itself
education:
  required: >
    High school diploma or equivalent, then trade school or a registered
    apprenticeship; for structural or pressure welding work, passing a
    certification test to the applicable welding code (most commonly AWS
    D1.1 for structural steel) for the specific process and position being
    performed, required by the code and the contract, not optional for
    that scope of work
  preferred: Additional process-specific certifications as project scopes require them
  notNecessary: A four-year college degree; trade school or apprenticeship leading to code certification is the standard path
progression:
  - Apprentice Welder
  - Certified Welder
  - Welding Foreman
  - Certified Welding Inspector (CWI) or Welding Contractor
hirerTypes:
  - Structural Steel Subcontractors
  - General Contractors (on projects that self-perform steel work)
  - Fabrication Shops
