title: Quantity Surveyor
category: "Preconstruction & Estimating"
tagline: Measures and values construction work in detail, a title that comes out of the UK/Commonwealth surveying tradition but increasingly shows up at U.S. firms with international ties.
tier: free
whatIs: >
  A Quantity Surveyor measures and values construction work throughout a
  project, similar territory to an Estimator and a Cost Engineer, with
  particular depth in precise quantity measurement and cost planning from
  early design through final account. The title and formal training
  structure originated in the UK, where the Royal Institution of Chartered
  Surveyors (RICS) governs a "Chartered Surveyor" (MRICS) credential; no
  U.S. state or federal license is required to use this title or do this
  work in the United States, so RICS chartership here is a professional
  recognition some practitioners pursue, not a legal requirement to work
  in the role.
whatTheyDo:
  - Prepare detailed cost estimates and bills of quantities from design documents
  - Track project costs from early design through final account, similar to a Cost Engineer
  - Advise on cost planning and value engineering options during design
  - Measure completed work for interim payment valuations
  - Assess the cost impact of variations and changes as they arise
  - Support contract administration around measurement-based payment terms
typicalDay:
  - time: "8:00 AM"
    activity: Prepare a bill of quantities from a newly issued set of drawings
  - time: "9:30 AM"
    activity: Review a subcontractor's payment valuation against measured quantities of completed work
  - time: "11:00 AM"
    activity: Advise the design team on a cost-saving alternative during a value engineering session
  - time: "1:30 PM"
    activity: Assess the cost impact of a design variation
  - time: "3:30 PM"
    activity: Update the project's cost plan with the latest measured quantities
whereTheyWork:
  - Office
  - Occasional site visits to verify measured quantities of completed work
skills:
  - Precise quantity measurement
  - Cost estimating and cost planning
  - Contract and payment administration
  - Attention to detail
  - Communication with designers, contractors, and owners

education:
  required: >
    High school diploma or equivalent, since no certification or license is
    legally required to work as a Quantity Surveyor in the United States
  preferred: Bachelor's degree in quantity surveying, construction management, or a related field
  helpful: RICS Chartered Surveyor (MRICS) status, mainly relevant at firms with UK/Commonwealth ties or international project work
  notNecessary: A specific quantity surveying degree isn't available at most U.S. schools; many U.S.-based practitioners come from an estimating or cost engineering background instead
progression:
