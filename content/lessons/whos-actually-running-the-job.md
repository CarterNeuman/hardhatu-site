title: "Who's Actually Running the Job"

sections:
  - content: >
      Walk onto almost any job site and you'll find the same three parties.
      The [[concept-owner|owner]] is paying for the project and will end up
      owning it, so every decision eventually comes back to what they want.
      The [[concept-architect|architect]] designs the project, and on most
      traditional jobs, also acts as a referee during construction, answering
      questions about what the drawings actually mean. The
      [[concept-general-contractor|general contractor]] holds the contract to
      actually build it and coordinates every trade doing the physical work.
      Three very different jobs, and only one of them is directly hiring the
      people swinging a hammer.
    quiz:
      question: "On a traditional project, what does the architect typically do besides design the building?"
      options:
        - "They also act as a referee during construction, answering questions about what the drawings actually mean"
        - "They hand off essentially every construction-phase decision to the general contractor's own team"
        - "They're hired and paid directly by the general contractor instead of by the project owner"
      answerIndex: 0
      explanation: >
        The architect's dual role, design plus referee, surprises a lot of
        newcomers. It's why design questions usually get routed to them, not
        to whoever's building it.
  - content: >
      Here's what actually decides who can tell whom what to do: contracts,
      not job titles. The owner has one contract, the
      [[concept-prime-contract|prime contract]] , and it's with the general
      contractor only. The GC then signs a separate
      [[concept-subcontract-agreement|subcontract agreement]] with each
      [[concept-subcontractor|subcontractor]] , one for electrical, one for
      drywall, and so on. That's why an owner can't just walk up to an
      electrical sub's foreman and start giving orders: there's no contract
      between them, so there's no legal weight behind it. Keeping instructions
      flowing through that chain is what keeps the GC accountable for the
      whole job instead of everyone quietly doing their own thing. The
      day-to-day rules for how that chain works usually live in the
      [[concept-general-conditions|general conditions]] , a standard form most
      projects use, with
      [[concept-supplementary-conditions|supplementary conditions]] added on
      top to fit the specific project.
    quiz:
      question: "An owner is frustrated with an electrical subcontractor and wants to tell their foreman directly what to change. Why doesn't that work?"
      options:
        - "The owner is paying for the whole project, which gives them automatic authority over every subcontractor on it"
        - "There's no contract between the owner and the subcontractor, so there's no legal weight behind the instruction"
        - "Electrical subcontractors are trained to only take direction from their own company's foreman, no matter who else is on site"
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        Contracts decide who can give binding instructions. The owner's
        contract is with the GC; the GC's contract is with the sub. Direction
        is supposed to follow that same chain.
  - content: >
      Most owners aren't in the construction business full time, which is
      exactly the gap an
      [[career-owners-representative|Owner's Representative]] fills. Hired by
      the owner and working only for the owner, they sit in meetings
      translating technical issues into decisions the owner can actually make.
      A [[career-contracts-administrator|Contracts Administrator]] handles the
      paperwork side of that same chain, drafting and managing every contract
      so the terms stay consistent and nothing gets left out. One more piece
      worth knowing: risk flows through that same chain too, through
      [[concept-indemnification|indemnification]] clauses, where a
      subcontractor agrees to cover claims from their own work, protecting the
      GC, who in turn protects the owner.
    quiz:
      question: "What's the real difference between an Owner's Representative and a General Contractor?"
      options:
        - "The GC is managing its own contract and scope; the Owner's Rep has no scope of their own and exists only to protect the owner"
        - "There's barely a difference between the two roles, since both report to the same schedule and share most of the same day-to-day duties"
        - "The Owner's Rep usually takes over scheduling duties from the GC once construction is fully underway on site"
      answerIndex: 0
      explanation: >
        Even a great GC still has its own contract and profit margin to
        manage. An Owner's Rep is brought in with no competing interest of
        their own.
  - content: >
      Everything so far assumes one owner, one GC, coordinating every sub
      underneath them. That's common, but not universal. Under
      [[concept-multiple-prime-contracting|multiple prime contracting]] , the
      owner contracts directly with several prime contractors instead, like a
      general prime and an electrical prime, with nobody automatically in
      charge of resolving conflicts between them. Whichever structure a
      project uses, someone has to track who asked what and which drawing is
      current, especially once RFIs start piling up. That's the
      [[career-document-control-specialist|Document Control Specialist]] 's
      job: keeping logs, distributing current drawings, and making sure nobody
      builds off information that's already out of date.
    quiz:
      question: "Under multiple prime contracting, who usually resolves a scheduling conflict between two prime contractors?"
      options:
        - "The two primes typically resolve it between themselves, the same way they would under a single GC"
        - "The owner, or whoever they've hired to coordinate, since no single prime has authority over another"
        - "Multiple prime contracting is designed specifically to prevent scheduling conflicts from happening at all"
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        Without one GC holding every contract, there's no built-in authority
        over all the trades. That job doesn't disappear, it just moves up to
        the owner.
  - content: >
      So what's the real answer to who do I ask? It's never about who sounds
      most confident on site. The architect's office answers questions about
      what the design means, often through a formal [[concept-rfi|RFI]] . The
      GC decides how the work actually proceeds. The owner, or their rep,
      weighs in on anything that changes cost or schedule. If untangling that
      kind of structure sounds interesting, the Owner's Representative,
      Contracts Administrator, and Document Control Specialist careers are all
      built around exactly this. The one thing worth remembering: on a job
      site, who can tell whom what to do always comes down to who actually has
      a contract with whom.
    quiz:
      question: "What actually determines who has authority to direct someone else's work on a job?"
      options:
        - "Whoever has the most seniority on site, regardless of which contract they're actually working under"
        - "Whoever sounds most confident and gives the most technical detail during that day's coordination meeting on site"
        - "The actual chain of contracts between the parties, since authority only flows between parties who signed with each other"
      answerIndex: 2
      explanation: >
        It's the theme of this whole lesson: authority runs on contracts, not
        personalities. Trace who has a contract with whom, and the confusion
        usually clears up.
