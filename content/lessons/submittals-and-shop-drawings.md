  - content: >
      Six weeks before a hotel's grand opening, a general contractor's project
      engineer gets a call from the millwork fabricator: the custom lobby reception
      desk is finished and ready for delivery. There's just one problem. The
      [[concept-shop-drawings|shop drawings]] for that desk are still sitting in the
      architect's review queue, stamped, as of yesterday, "revise and resubmit," over
      a dimension that doesn't actually match the electrical rough-in behind it. The
      fabricator built the whole thing anyway, betting the drawings would get approved
      as submitted. They didn't, and now an expensive, custom piece of millwork needs
      to be reworked, or worse, rebuilt, on a schedule with no slack left in it.

      That fabricator built at risk, ordering and fabricating before the approval loop
      actually closed, and it's one of the single most expensive mistakes a
      subcontractor or supplier can make on a project. The
      [[concept-submittal|submittal]] process exists precisely to prevent this: shop
      drawings, product data, and samples get reviewed and approved by the architect
      or engineer before anything gets ordered or built, confirming what's about to
      happen actually matches what was designed. Understanding that process well
      enough to actually manage it, not just recognize the paperwork, is what this
      lesson is really about.
    quiz:
      question: What does it mean for a subcontractor to be "fabricating at risk"?
      options:
        - Fabricating a product before its submittal has actually been approved, betting it will pass review
        - Choosing to install a slightly different material than the approved specification calls for, without notifying the design team first
        - Continuing fabrication work during a scheduled safety stand-down instead of pausing operations like the rest of the crew
      answerIndex: 0
      explanation: >
        Fabricating at risk means starting real, expensive work before the review
        loop has actually closed. When it pays off, nobody notices; when it doesn't,
        the cost of rework or a rebuild usually lands on whoever jumped ahead.

  - content: >
      It's easy for a newcomer to mix up an [[concept-rfi|RFI]] and a submittal,
      since both move through similar-looking logs and both involve sending something
      to the design team and waiting on an answer. They're actually solving two
      different problems. An RFI gets filed when the drawings or specs themselves are
      unclear, missing, or contradictory, a genuine question that needs an answer
      before work can proceed correctly. A submittal isn't a question at all: it's a
      confirmation, sent after the design intent is already clear, showing exactly
      what a contractor intends to install and asking the design team to verify it
      actually matches.

      Put another way: an RFI exists because something in the documents doesn't make
      sense yet; a submittal exists because something does make sense, and now needs
      to be checked against reality before it gets built. Confusing the two in the
      field usually shows up as a subcontractor treating a genuinely ambiguous
      drawing as if it were already settled, or treating a routine product
      confirmation as if it needed a full design decision, both of which slow a
      project down for the wrong reason.
    quiz:
      question: >
        A drawing shows a wall in two different places on two different sheets, and
        nobody can tell which one is correct. Is this an RFI or a submittal
        situation?
      options:
        - A submittal, since the wall itself is a physical building component that needs to be confirmed and verified before construction
        - An RFI, since the design intent itself is unclear and needs an answer before work proceeds
        - Neither formally; a conflict like this would typically just get talked through verbally on site without needing any written documentation
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        A submittal only makes sense once the design intent is already clear. Here,
        the drawings themselves contradict each other, which is exactly the kind of
        ambiguity an RFI exists to resolve first.

  - content: >
      Once a submittal actually reaches the architect or engineer, it doesn't just
      come back approved or rejected. There's real range in between, and knowing the
      difference matters. "Approved" means proceed exactly as submitted. "Approved as
      noted" means proceed, but incorporate the specific corrections the reviewer
      marked, without needing to send it back through another full review cycle.
      "Revise and resubmit" means stop: the submittal doesn't accurately represent
      what should be built yet, and a corrected version has to come back through
      review before anything moves forward, exactly the stamp the lobby desk's shop
      drawings came back with. An outright rejection means the wrong product or
      approach entirely, back to the drawing board.

      Every one of these exchanges travels with a
      [[concept-transmittal|transmittal]], a cover document recording exactly what
      was sent, to whom, and when, independent of the submittal itself. It sounds
      like paperwork for paperwork's sake until a schedule dispute breaks out over
      whether a submittal was actually received on time, at which point the
      transmittal, not the memory of whoever sent it, is what actually settles the
      question.
    quiz:
      question: A submittal comes back stamped "approved as noted." What should happen next?
      options:
        - Work has to stop completely until the fabricator throws out the current submittal and starts an entirely new one from scratch
        - The reviewer's handwritten notes are mostly optional guidance, so the contractor can move ahead treating the item as fully approved without changes
        - The contractor can proceed, incorporating the reviewer's specific corrections, without resubmitting for another full review
      answerIndex: 2
      explanation: >
        "Approved as noted" is a green light with conditions attached. It's meant to
        keep the project moving while still requiring the noted corrections actually
        get built in, not treated as optional.

  - content: >
      Not every submittal can wait its turn in the normal project schedule. A
      [[concept-long-lead-item|long-lead item]], custom elevators, switchgear,
      specialty mechanical equipment, can take months to manufacture and deliver,
      sometimes far longer than it would take to design and build everything else
      around it. If the submittal and approval process for that one item doesn't
      happen early, well before the rest of the project would normally need it, that
      single piece of equipment can end up dictating the entire project's completion
      date, no matter how efficiently everything else gets built.

      Recognizing a long-lead item early, and pushing its submittal to the front of
      the review queue rather than letting it wait its natural turn, is one of the
      more consequential judgment calls a project team makes. Miss it, and a building
      can sit essentially finished for months, waiting on one piece of equipment that
      should have been ordered before the foundation was even poured.
    quiz:
      question: >
        Why does a long-lead item sometimes get submitted for approval before most
        of a project's other design details are even finalized?
      options:
        - Because its long manufacturing and delivery time means ordering it late can delay the entire project, regardless of how quickly everything else gets built
        - Because long-lead items generally end up being the most budget-friendly option once a project team compares every available supplier's pricing
        - Because building codes specifically require certain equipment submittals to be fast-tracked ahead of the rest of a project's design documents
      answerIndex: 0
      explanation: >
        A long-lead item's own manufacturing time, not the rest of the project's
        pace, is what actually sets the deadline for getting its submittal approved
        and the order placed.

  - content: >
      Back to that lobby desk. What actually should have happened is straightforward
      in hindsight: hold fabrication until the submittal cycle genuinely closes,
      whether that's a clean approval, an "approved as noted" the fabricator can
      build from immediately, or a "revise and resubmit" that gets corrected and
      cleared before a single board gets cut. None of that requires special
      expertise, just the discipline to treat "still in review" as exactly what it
      says, not yet approved, rather than a formality to build around.

      Keeping all of it straight, which submittals are moving, which are overdue,
      which are long-lead items that need to jump the queue, is exactly what a
      [[concept-submittal-log|submittal log]] exists to track, and it's a defining
      part of a [[career-project-engineer|Project Engineer]]'s job on most projects.
      It's also exactly why the role is such a common entry point into project
      management: the person who actually understands the state of every submittal
      on a job usually understands the state of the whole project.

      If you remember one thing from this lesson, make it this: a submittal isn't
      paperwork standing between a contractor and the real work, it's part of the
      real work, the actual mechanism that keeps a hundred different decisions made
      months apart from quietly drifting out of sync with each other. Skip the loop
      to save a few days, and the fabricator with the lobby desk is proof of exactly
      how expensive that shortcut can get.
    quiz:
      question: >
        What's the common thread connecting RFIs, submittal review stamps,
        transmittals, and long-lead item scheduling?
      options:
        - They're mostly redundant paperwork systems that duplicate the same information, kept in place more out of habit than because each one serves a distinct purpose
        - Each one is a mechanism for keeping what actually gets built in sync with what was actually designed and approved, and for proving that sync happened when questions come up later
        - These tracking systems mostly matter on very large commercial projects, with smaller residential and light commercial jobs rarely needing this level of documentation
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        From resolving ambiguity to verifying a specific product to proving exactly
        when something was sent, every piece of this lesson exists to keep design
        intent and field reality from quietly drifting apart, and to leave a record
        when they do.
