title: "How Getting Paid Actually Works"

sections:
  - content: >
      Picture yourself as a contracts administrator at a general contracting firm,
      three weeks into a new hospital wing project. Before any subcontractor sees a
      dollar, they submit a [[concept-schedule-of-values|schedule of values]], a
      breakdown of their lump-sum contract into individual line items, each with its
      own dollar value: rough-in electrical, fixture installation, panel work, and so
      on. That breakdown becomes the backbone of every payment that follows.

      Each month, the electrical subcontractor submits a payment application showing
      what percentage of each line item is actually complete, twenty percent of
      rough-in, none of fixtures yet. Your job is to verify that against what the
      superintendent actually saw in the field this month, not just take the sub's word
      for it. A subcontractor who bills sixty percent complete on a line item the
      superintendent walked and found at thirty percent isn't automatically committing
      fraud, sometimes it's an honest disagreement about what counts as "complete," but
      it's exactly the kind of gap this monthly verification exists to catch before
      real money changes hands on a number nobody actually confirmed.
    quiz:
      question: What does a schedule of values actually do?
      options:
        - It sets the final total price of the contract, nothing more
        - It breaks a lump-sum contract into individual line items with their own dollar values, giving every future payment application something concrete to measure progress against
        - It's a one-time document that has no bearing on later payments
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        Without a schedule of values, "how much of this contract is done" would have
        no agreed structure to measure against. It's what turns a lump sum into
        something payable in verifiable monthly pieces.

  - content: >
      Once a pay application is approved, the subcontractor doesn't actually receive
      the full amount. Like most contracts on this project, theirs includes
      [[concept-retainage|retainage]], typically five to ten percent withheld from
      every payment and held until the work is substantially complete. It isn't a
      penalty, it's leverage: it gives the general contractor a real financial reason
      to make sure a subcontractor actually finishes their punch list items and fixes
      any defects, rather than collecting full payment and moving on to the next job
      with loose ends still hanging.

      The electrical subcontractor's owner isn't thrilled about it, ten percent held
      back across an entire project is real cash flow sitting somewhere else, but
      understands why it exists. What frustrates subcontractors more is when retainage
      gets held past substantial completion for unrelated reasons, since the whole
      point of the practice is to protect against unfinished work, not to function as
      free financing for whoever's holding it.
    quiz:
      question: What is retainage actually meant to accomplish?
      options:
        - It's simply a penalty charged to every subcontractor regardless of performance
        - It gives the party holding it real financial leverage to ensure a subcontractor genuinely finishes their work and fixes defects before getting fully paid
        - It has no real purpose and exists only out of habit
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        Retainage is functional leverage, not a punishment. Holding back a percentage
        of every payment keeps a real financial incentive in place until the work is
        actually finished.

  - content: >
      This particular hospital wing is a large private project, and even though nothing
      here legally requires it the way public work would, the hospital system's lender
      insists on the same bonding most public projects carry by law: a
      [[concept-performance-bond|performance bond]] and a
      [[concept-payment-bond|payment bond]], each protecting a different party from a
      different failure. What matters for the payment chain specifically is simpler
      than the bond mechanics themselves: if the general contractor can't finish the
      job or can't pay its subcontractors, the money to make everyone whole still
      exists somewhere, backed by a surety rather than depending on whatever cash the
      general contractor happens to have on hand at that moment.

      That's exactly why a lender with no direct stake in the construction itself still
      insists on it: bonding is what keeps one contractor's financial failure from
      becoming everyone downstream's unpaid invoice, on a private job just as much as
      on public work that requires it by law.
    quiz:
      question: Why would a hospital system's lender, with no direct role in the construction itself, insist on the same bonding a public project would carry by law?
      options:
        - Lenders are legally required to insist on bonding for any project they finance
        - Bonding keeps the general contractor's financial failure from becoming an unpaid invoice for everyone downstream, protecting the lender's own investment in the finished building either way
        - Bonding is purely a formality with no real financial function on a private project
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        A lender's real exposure is the same as the owner's: an unfinished or
        legally tangled project is bad for everyone with money in it. Bonding backs
        the payment chain with a surety instead of leaving it dependent on the
        general contractor's own cash position.

  - content: >
      Two months later, the general contractor itself is waiting on a slow payment
      from the hospital's ownership group, and the electrical subcontractor's most
      recent invoice sits unpaid past its due date. The contract language matters here:
      a [[concept-pay-when-paid|pay-when-paid clause]] in the subcontract shifts the
      risk of the owner's slow payment down onto the subcontractor, meaning the
      subcontractor may have to wait even though their own work is done and approved.

      Frustrated and needing cash flow, the subcontractor's owner threatens to file a
      [[concept-mechanics-lien|mechanic's lien]] against the property itself, a legal
      claim that clouds the title and can block the owner from selling or refinancing
      until the debt is resolved. It's a genuinely serious escalation, not an idle
      threat: liens exist precisely because a subcontractor who does real work has real
      leverage to get paid, even when they're several contractual layers removed from
      the property owner who ultimately controls the money.
    quiz:
      question: Why would an unpaid subcontractor's threat to file a mechanic's lien be taken seriously, rather than dismissed as an empty threat?
      options:
        - It wouldn't be, liens have no real legal effect on a property
        - A mechanic's lien clouds the property's title and can block a sale or refinance until resolved, giving an unpaid subcontractor real leverage even without a direct contract with the owner
        - Mechanic's liens can only be filed by the general contractor, never a subcontractor
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        A lien attaches to the property itself, not just to whichever contract went
        unpaid. That's exactly what gives a subcontractor real leverage even when the
        person who actually owes them money is having their own payment problems.

  - content: >
      The contracts administrator steps in before the lien actually gets filed,
      pushing the general contractor's own payment through faster than the pay-when-paid
      clause technically requires, since an actual lien on a hospital's title is a
      genuinely bad outcome for everyone involved, not just the subcontractor. Once
      payment clears, the subcontractor signs a [[concept-lien-waiver|lien waiver]],
      a document confirming payment was received in exchange for formally giving up any
      lien rights tied to that payment. On this project, as on most, a lien waiver gets
      signed and collected at every single payment stage, not just the final one, so
      nobody's exposure to an old, already-paid invoice ever quietly lingers.

      Getting paid on a construction project was never really one transaction. It's a
      chain: a schedule of values sets the terms, a pay application measures progress
      against it, retainage and bonds manage risk along the way, and every payment gets
      formally closed out with a waiver, each link depending on the ones before it
      actually holding. The [[career-contracts-administrator|Contracts Administrator]]
      and [[career-construction-accountant|Construction Accountant]] run this system
      day to day, the [[career-project-manager|Project Manager]] leans on it constantly
      when negotiating change orders or managing subcontractor relationships, and a
      [[career-construction-attorney|Construction Attorney]] gets pulled in the moment
      a dispute like a threatened lien escalates past what the contracts administrator
      can resolve directly. The [[interview-business|Business interview guide]] and the
      [[interview-project-manager|Project Manager interview guide]] both cover what
      these conversations actually test for.

      If you remember one thing from this lesson, make it this: money on a construction
      project doesn't move in one step, it moves through a chain of documents, and a
      weak link anywhere in that chain, an unverified pay application, an ignored
      retainage dispute, a missed lien deadline, becomes real financial exposure for
      whoever's standing closest to it when it breaks.
    quiz:
      question: What's the underlying theme this lesson draws across schedules of values, retainage, bonds, liens, and lien waivers?
      options:
        - They're unrelated documents that happen to all involve money
        - Getting paid on a construction project is a connected chain of documents and protections, and a weak or skipped link anywhere in that chain becomes real financial risk for someone
        - Only the final lien waiver actually matters; everything before it is just paperwork
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        Every document in this lesson exists to protect against a specific failure
        point in the payment chain. Skip or mishandle any one of them, and the risk it
        was managing doesn't disappear, it just lands on whoever's exposed when it
        surfaces.
