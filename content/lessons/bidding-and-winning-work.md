title: "How a Project Actually Gets Bid and Won"
tier: free
minutes: 11
sections:
  - content: >
      Picture yourself as an estimator at a mid-size contractor. Before anyone
      talks price, the owner's team needs to know your firm can actually pull
      the job off. That screening usually starts with a
      [[concept-request-for-qualifications|Request for Qualifications (RFQ)]]
      , asking about your firm's past projects, finances, and safety record,
      no pricing yet. Out of a dozen firms, a few get shortlisted, and only
      then does the real ask land: a
      [[concept-request-for-proposal|Request for Proposal (RFP)]] , asking for
      your team, your approach, and finally, a price. Public work often skips
      that qualifications step entirely and goes straight to an
      [[concept-invitation-for-bid|Invitation for Bid (IFB)]] , a stricter,
      price-only process where the contract has to go to the lowest qualified
      bidder by law. Some public agencies narrow things further with a
      [[concept-prequalification|prequalified]] bidders list, screened in
      advance so nobody has to evaluate a bid from a firm that couldn't
      actually do the work.
    quiz:
      question: "A public IFB has one rule above almost everything else. What is it?"
      options:
        - "The contract must go to the lowest bid that meets the stated requirements, with almost no room to weigh qualifications"
        - "The owner has fairly wide discretion to pick whichever contractor's team and approach they personally like best"
        - "Whichever bidder has completed the greatest number of similar projects in the past almost always wins the contract"
      answerIndex: 0
      explanation: >
        An IFB is deliberately price-driven and rule-bound, partly to avoid
        favoritism. That's very different from an RFP, which is built
        specifically to weigh qualifications and approach alongside price.
  - content: >
      Say your firm makes the RFP shortlist. Before anyone can put a number on
      paper, contractors walk the actual building together at a
      [[concept-pre-bid-meeting|pre-bid meeting]] . Answers given there, like
      where a hazard is or what's actually included, can swing every bidder's
      price by thousands of dollars. If an answer or a mistake is significant
      enough, the architect sends every bidder a formal
      [[concept-addendum|addendum]] , a numbered correction, so nobody prices
      a different project than everyone else. Missing one is one of the most
      common, avoidable ways a bid goes wrong. Back at the office, pricing
      starts with a [[concept-quantity-takeoff|quantity takeoff]] , measuring
      exactly how much of everything the job needs straight off the drawings.
      Get the takeoff wrong, and every price built on top of it is wrong too.
    quiz:
      question: "Why does an estimator attend the pre-bid meeting in person instead of just working from the drawings?"
      options:
        - "Simply showing up to the pre-bid meeting in person essentially guarantees that a firm's proposal will be the one that wins"
        - "Attending the pre-bid meeting is mostly a formality estimators go through out of habit, with little real impact on the final price"
        - "Real site conditions, like access, hazards, or layout quirks, can change the price significantly and drawings don't always show them"
      answerIndex: 2
      explanation: >
        Drawings only tell part of the story. A contractor who skips the
        walk-through is pricing blind on real conditions that every attending
        bidder now knows to account for.
  - content: >
      A common assumption worth correcting: the lowest number doesn't always
      win. On a public IFB, it basically does, by law. On an RFP, it doesn't,
      since qualifications and approach count too. Before your firm's
      [[concept-bid|bid]] can even go in, it usually needs a
      [[concept-bid-bond|bid bond]] , a guarantee from a bonding company that
      if you win, you'll actually sign the contract. Bonding capacity is real
      and limited, not a rubber stamp, so a firm bidding several big jobs at
      once can get stuck unable to bond the one it actually wins. Once
      subcontractor quotes come in, they rarely match up cleanly, one might
      exclude something another includes.
      [[concept-bid-leveling|Bid leveling]] fixes that: adjusting every quote
      to reflect the same actual scope before anyone compares numbers. Skip
      it, and the savings from a cheaper-looking quote tend to come back later
      as a change order.
    quiz:
      question: "Two drywall subcontractors quote the same job, but one number is $8,000 lower. What should an estimator check first?"
      options:
        - "Nothing further really needs checking, since the lower of two quotes for the same described scope is almost always the better deal"
        - "Whether both quotes actually include the same scope of work, since an excluded item can make a quote look artificially cheap"
        - "Whether the subcontractor offering the lower number is willing to negotiate their price down even further before signing"
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        This is exactly what bid leveling exists to catch. A cheaper quote
        that's missing scope isn't actually cheaper, and the missing piece
        usually shows up later as a change order.
  - content: >
      Winning the job isn't the finish line. The number that won it was built
      from estimated subcontractor costs, some quoted under real time
      pressure. [[concept-buyout|Buyout]] is the process right after award of
      turning those estimates into actual signed subcontracts, and the gap
      between the two often decides whether the project hits its profit
      margin. Every one of those negotiations gets measured against the
      [[concept-scope-of-work|scope of work]] , the written description of
      exactly what's included in the price, and what isn't. Almost every
      payment dispute eventually comes back to that same question. And
      sometimes the numbers just don't work at all: the bid comes in over what
      the owner can spend. That's when
      [[concept-value-engineering|value engineering]] comes in, a systematic
      search for a cheaper material or method that still gives the owner what
      they actually need.
    quiz:
      question: "A subcontractor's buyout price for a specialty scope comes in $30,000 over what was carried in the original winning bid. What does that mean?"
      options:
        - "The general contractor can generally pass that $30,000 difference along to the owner as a billing adjustment on the next invoice"
        - "Nothing changes from the owner's side, since the original bid number carries the only legal weight once the contract is signed"
        - "That $30,000 gap eats directly into the project's profit margin, since the contract price with the owner doesn't change"
      answerIndex: 2
      explanation: >
        The price the owner is paying was locked in at bid. If buyout comes in
        over what was carried, that difference comes straight out of the
        contractor's own margin.
  - content: >
      Zoom out, and the whole process runs in order: qualifications get
      screened first, then the real ask goes out, then contractors walk the
      site and get corrections in writing, then the number gets built from the
      ground up, then competing quotes get leveled before anyone picks a
      winner, and even after signing, buyout decides what it actually costs.
      This is the daily world of an [[career-estimator|Estimator]] and
      [[career-senior-estimator|Senior Estimator]] , often supported by a
      [[career-bid-coordinator|Bid Coordinator]] and a
      [[career-preconstruction-manager|Preconstruction Manager]] . If this
      sounds like your kind of work, the
      [[interview-preconstruction-estimating|Preconstruction & Estimating interview guide]]
      and the [[exam-cpe|Certified Professional Estimator (CPE) exam guide]]
      are good next steps. One thing worth remembering: almost every mistake
      in this lesson is the same mistake wearing a different hat, someone
      skipping a step right before the exact problem that step exists to
      catch.
    quiz:
      question: "What's the common thread connecting most of the bidding mistakes in this lesson?"
      options:
        - "Contractors intentionally trying to game the bidding system for their own advantage rather than making an honest mistake"
        - "Mostly bad luck that can't really be prevented no matter how carefully an estimator or project manager plans the bid"
        - "Someone skipping a specific verification step under time pressure, right before the exact problem that step exists to catch"
      answerIndex: 2
      explanation: >
        From missing an addendum to skipping bid leveling to rushing buyout,
        the pattern repeats: a step built to catch a risk gets skipped to save
        time, and the risk shows up anyway, later and more expensively.
