sections:
  - content: >
      Hand someone new a full set of construction drawings, and the reaction
      is always the same: a thick stack of sheets with no idea where to even
      look. Good news: drawing sets aren't random. Every sheet starts with a
      letter, called a discipline designator, that tells you which trade it
      belongs to. G is General. C is Civil, the site and utilities. S is
      Structural, the frame and foundation. A is Architectural, the floor
      plans and finishes most people picture when they hear blueprints. M, E,
      and P cover Mechanical, Electrical, and Plumbing. Learn that one letter,
      and you already know which sheets to open for almost any question.
    quiz:
      question: "What does the letter at the start of a sheet number, like the S in S-201, actually tell you?"
      options:
        - "The exact date the sheet was last revised, which is really tracked in a separate revision block instead"
        - "Which discipline that sheet belongs to, like Structural or Architectural, following one standard convention"
        - "Which architectural firm or engineering consultant produced that sheet, mainly used for internal billing"
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        The discipline letter is one of the most useful things to learn early.
        Once you can read it, you can navigate an unfamiliar drawing set
        almost right away.
  - content: >
      The rest of the sheet number follows a pattern too. A number like A-101
      breaks down into the discipline letter, a digit for the sheet type, 1
      for plans, 2 for elevations, 3 for sections, 5 for details, and a
      sequence number after that. A plan shows where things go from overhead.
      An elevation shows what a wall looks like from the side. A section is a
      vertical slice straight through the building. None of these views alone
      tells the whole story, which is why a good reader checks the plan, then
      the section, then the detail before assuming any one sheet has the full
      answer.
    quiz:
      question: "You need to see exactly how a window sill is supposed to be built, layer by layer. Which sheet do you look for?"
      options:
        - "A plan sheet, since the overhead view already shows roughly where each window sits along the wall"
        - "A detail sheet, since details show the zoomed-in, fabrication-level information a plan or elevation leaves out"
        - "An elevation sheet, since it already shows the wall's finished outside appearance in enough detail to build from"
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        Plans and elevations show where things are and what they look like
        from a distance. Details are where the actual how-it-goes-together
        information lives.
  - content: >
      A lot of newcomers are surprised to learn the architect's drawings
      aren't actually what most trades build from directly. They show design
      intent, not fabrication instructions. A steel fabricator takes the
      architect's drawings and produces their own
      [[concept-shop-drawings|shop drawings]] , detailed enough to show exact
      bolt patterns and connections. The architect or engineer has to review
      and approve that translation before anything gets ordered, through the
      [[concept-submittal|submittal]] process. On a project of any real size,
      a [[concept-submittal-log|submittal log]] tracks every one of those
      approvals so nothing quietly misses its deadline. For especially risky
      or visible work, a [[concept-mock-up|mock-up]] gets built first, a
      full-scale sample approved before the same thing gets repeated across
      the whole building.
    quiz:
      question: "Why does a steel fabricator produce their own shop drawings instead of just building straight from the architect's drawings?"
      options:
        - "The architect's drawings show design intent, not fabrication-level detail like exact bolt patterns and connections"
        - "Shop drawings are mainly required so the general contractor has a complete paper trail to bill the owner for each piece"
        - "Fabricators redraw the architect's sheets at a larger scale mainly so the drawings are easier for a field crew to read"
      answerIndex: 0
      explanation: >
        An architect's drawings communicate what the finished building should
        be. A fabricator still has to figure out and document exactly how
        their specific piece gets built and connected.
  - content: >
      Reading plans well matters beyond just building from them. Before
      construction can legally start, the local building department checks the
      drawings against the [[concept-building-code|building code]] , a process
      called [[concept-plan-review|plan review]] . Catching a code problem
      here, on paper, is far cheaper than catching it after it's actually
      built. Passing plan review is what earns a project its
      [[concept-building-permit|building permit]] , the government sign-off
      required before most work can start. Because code requirements differ by
      city, a [[career-permit-expediter|Permit Expediter]] exists specifically
      to push permit applications through review without stalling the whole
      project.
    quiz:
      question: "What is plan review actually checking a set of drawings against?"
      options:
        - "The applicable building code, catching potential code problems on paper before construction starts"
        - "The project's total budget and whether the contractor's bid actually matches what the owner agreed to pay"
        - "Whether the building's design fits in with the surrounding neighborhood, usually handled by a separate review board"
      answerIndex: 0
      explanation: >
        Plan review is a code compliance check, not a design critique or a
        budget review. It pushes the discovery of a code problem as early as
        possible, back when it's still just a line on a drawing.
  - content: >
      Once construction starts, the plans keep evolving on purpose. Field
      conditions rarely match a drawing exactly, so those changes get folded
      into a final set of [[concept-as-built-drawings|as-built drawings]] ,
      the record of what actually got built. For work that becomes invisible
      once it's covered, like a weld inside a column, drawings alone can't
      verify anything after the fact. That's what
      [[concept-special-inspection|special inspection]] is for: an independent
      check, in real time, of the work that holds a building up. Many projects
      now coordinate all of this digitally too, guided by a
      [[concept-bim-execution-plan|BIM execution plan]] . Keeping all of it
      straight, drawing revisions, submittal status, RFI answers, is usually a
      [[career-project-engineer|Project Engineer]] 's first real job, and it's
      exactly why the role is such a common way into project management. Learn
      to navigate a drawing set, and it stops being an intimidating stack of
      paper and starts being the most reliable answer on a job site.
    quiz:
      question: "What's the common thread connecting plan review, submittals, and special inspection?"
      options:
        - "They're all performed by the general contractor's own quality control team, separate from the architect or engineer"
        - "They're all steps mainly required on large public projects, with most private commercial work able to skip them"
        - "Each one catches a specific kind of mistake on paper, or independently in the field, before it's expensive to fix"
      answerIndex: 2
      explanation: >
        From code compliance to fabrication accuracy to critical structural
        work, the pattern repeats: catching a problem before or during
        construction is far cheaper than finding it after the fact.
