sections:
  - content: >
      Picture yourself as a
      [[career-preconstruction-manager|Preconstruction Manager]] called in
      before a single drawing exists, sitting across the table from a developer
      who wants a straight answer: is this vacant parcel actually worth
      building on. [[phase-idea-feasibility|Idea and feasibility]] is the
      quiet, mostly invisible phase where that question gets answered, and the
      rough order-of-magnitude budget built there is deliberately closer to an
      educated guess than a real estimate, because its only job is telling the
      owner whether the idea is in the right financial neighborhood before
      anyone spends real money finding out the hard way. Getting that rough
      number wrong in either direction is costly. Too optimistic, and the
      project moves forward on a budget it can never actually meet. Too
      conservative, and a genuinely viable project gets walked away from for no
      real reason.
    quiz:
      question: "Why is the rough order-of-magnitude budget built during feasibility deliberately closer to an educated guess than a real estimate?"
      options:
        - "Because lenders require every early-stage budget to be rounded to the nearest hundred thousand dollars, regardless of how much design detail is actually available"
        - "Because its job is telling the owner whether an idea is in the right financial neighborhood before real money is spent"
        - "Because the detailed cost data a precise estimate would need, like subcontractor quotes and finalized drawings, typically doesn't exist until much later in design"
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        A rough order-of-magnitude budget exists to catch a clearly nonviable
        idea cheaply, not to price the project precisely; that precision comes
        later, once the idea has already cleared this first gut check.
  - content: >
      Even a financially sound idea can run straight into a wall that has
      nothing to do with money. [[concept-zoning-variances|Zoning]] governs
      what can legally be built on a given parcel, height limits, setbacks from
      the property line, how the land is allowed to be used, and a project that
      is fully viable on paper can still be legally impossible to build if it
      does not fit those rules as written. When it does not, the owner can
      apply for a variance, a specific, formally granted exception, but that
      process means public hearings and real negotiation with a local board,
      adding schedule risk a budget alone can never capture. An
      [[career-owners-representative|Owner's Representative]] often manages
      exactly this process on the owner's behalf, because confirming a site's
      zoning fits the project early, before a variance becomes the only
      remaining option, is far cheaper than discovering the conflict after
      design is already underway.
    quiz:
      question: "Why can a financially viable project still be legally impossible to build?"
      options:
        - "Because building code and zoning are reviewed by the same local department at the same time, so a project that passes one review has effectively already cleared the other"
        - "Because zoning governs what can legally be built on a given site, height, use, and setbacks, independent of whether the project actually makes financial sense"
        - "Because zoning requirements mainly come into play once a project has already secured its construction financing, rather than earlier during the feasibility phase"
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        Zoning regulates permitted use, height, and setbacks separately from
        building code; a design that violates those rules needs a formally
        granted variance, a process involving public hearings that adds real
        schedule risk.
  - content: >
      A clean zoning picture still is not enough on its own, because the ground
      itself can carry its own kind of risk. An
      [[concept-environmental-site-assessment|Environmental Site Assessment]]
      is a standardized review of a property's contamination history, starting
      with a non-intrusive Phase I review of records and past use, and
      escalating to actual soil and groundwater sampling in a Phase II only if
      that first review flags a real concern. What makes this particular
      assessment unusual among feasibility steps is that it is as much a
      financing requirement as an environmental one. A lender typically will
      not finance a purchase without a clean Phase I on file, so skipping it
      does not just leave an environmental question unanswered, it can keep the
      entire deal from closing at all, regardless of whether contamination
      actually exists on the site.
    quiz:
      question: "Why does a Phase I Environmental Site Assessment function as much as a financing requirement as an environmental one?"
      options:
        - "Because a lender financing the purchase typically will not close the loan without a clean Phase I assessment on file, separate from whatever the site itself shows"
        - "Because a Phase I assessment is the step that formally transfers legal liability for any contamination from the seller to the buyer once it's filed"
        - "Because a Phase I assessment is required mainly on projects receiving government funding, with privately financed deals typically able to skip it"
      answerIndex: 0
      explanation: >
        The assessment's financing role is independent of the actual
        environmental finding, since a missing or incomplete Phase I can keep a
        lender from closing the deal at all, separate from whatever the site
        itself turns out to show.
  - content: >
      Once the site itself clears zoning and environmental review, the money
      question still has to clear its own test, and the tool for that is a pro
      forma. A pro forma is a projected financial model built during
      feasibility that lays out a project's expected costs against its expected
      returns, such as rental income for an apartment building, lease revenue
      for a warehouse, or resale value for a spec house, over a multi-year
      horizon rather than just a single construction budget. Building that
      model is usually the developer's own financial team's work, not the
      construction side of the project, since it leans on real estate
      underwriting assumptions the actual construction crew never touches. A
      lender or investor reviews that pro forma alongside the rough budget
      before committing anything, because a project can be perfectly buildable
      and still be a bad investment if the numbers simply do not clear the
      return an investor actually needs to justify the risk. Once construction
      actually starts, a
      [[career-construction-accountant|Construction Accountant]] takes over
      tracking whether the real costs are landing anywhere near what that
      original pro forma assumed, the same job-costing discipline applied to a
      developer's long-range financial model instead of just a single project's
      budget.
    quiz:
      question: "What does a pro forma evaluate that a rough construction budget alone does not?"
      options:
        - "Whether the project's expected returns over time actually justify its costs, not just whether the budget covers construction itself"
        - "Whether the contractor's crew currently has enough labor hours available across its other active jobs to actually finish this one on schedule"
        - "Whether the site's soil and groundwater conditions, uncovered during the environmental review, require a more expensive foundation design than originally assumed"
      answerIndex: 0
      explanation: >
        A pro forma projects a project's expected costs against its expected
        returns over a multi-year horizon, which is how a lender or investor
        judges whether a buildable project is also a worthwhile investment.
  - content: >
      A pro forma that clears an investor's hurdle does not hand the project a
      lump sum of cash on day one. Construction financing typically moves
      through a draw schedule, where the lender releases money in stages,
      called draws, as the project actually demonstrates progress, rather than
      funding the full loan amount upfront against nothing but a set of
      drawings. Before releasing each draw, a lender typically sends its own
      inspector to verify the work claimed is actually in place, a check that
      runs alongside, but separately from, the
      [[concept-pay-application|pay applications]] the contractor is already
      submitting to the owner for the same work. A project that looked
      financially sound on paper during feasibility can still stall badly if
      its draw schedule and its actual construction pace fall out of sync. That
      is exactly why the careful, unglamorous work
      [[lesson-the-estimate-becomes-the-budget|tracking cost against budget]]
      throughout construction and the rough feasibility math that opened this
      lesson are really the same discipline, applied at two very different
      points in a project's life. For readers drawn to this side of the
      industry, the HardHatU [[interview-business|Business interview guide]]
      has more on how preconstruction, accounting, and ownership-side roles
      divide up this kind of work.
    quiz:
      question: "How does a construction loan typically get released to the project, and what checks that release?"
      options:
        - "In staged draws tied to demonstrated progress, each one checked by a lender's own inspector alongside the contractor's separate pay applications"
        - "In a single disbursement released once the project reaches substantial completion, with the lender relying entirely on the contractor's own pay applications until then"
        - "As a single lump sum released on day one of construction, based entirely on the pro forma the lender reviewed during underwriting"
      answerIndex: 0
      explanation: >
        A lender releases financing in draws as work actually progresses,
        sending its own inspector to verify each draw's claimed progress
        separately from, though alongside, the pay applications the contractor
        submits to the owner.
