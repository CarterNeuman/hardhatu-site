  - content: >
      Picture yourself as a [[career-project-manager|Project Manager]] three
      weeks into mobilization on a mid-rise renovation, reading the electrical
      subcontractor's scope exclusions against the mechanical subcontractor's
      scope exclusions side by side. Both documents look clean on their own.
      Read together, they reveal a gap: the electrical sub excluded control
      wiring for the new rooftop units, assuming the mechanical sub's price
      covered it, while the mechanical sub excluded exactly the same wiring,
      assuming it fell under electrical. Nobody lied and nobody missed a line
      item. Two [[concept-scope-of-work|scopes of work]] were each internally
      consistent and still left a real piece of the building with no
      subcontractor responsible for installing it.
      [[lesson-bidding-and-winning-work|Winning the bid]] answers who gets the
      job. A separate, quieter discipline decides whether every piece of that
      job actually lands on someone's price, and figuring that out before steel
      goes up is a core part of the job for the people who run it.
    quiz:
      question: "What made the rooftop-unit control wiring a true scope gap rather than a missed line item?"
      options:
        - "The project's drawings showed the rooftop units but left their control wiring routing unresolved between trades"
        - "Both subcontractors' scopes of work excluded the same piece of work, each reasonably assuming the other covered it"
        - "The electrical subcontractor's bid left out the control wiring because of a pricing error during its final estimate review"
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        Each subcontractor's exclusions were internally consistent and
        individually reasonable; the gap existed only because the two scopes,
        read together, left the work with nobody responsible for it.
  - content: >
      The fix for a gap like that starts with the subcontract itself, not with
      a phone call. Most subcontracts carry a
      [[concept-flow-down-clause|flow-down clause]], language that pulls the
      relevant obligations from the owner's prime contract with the general
      contractor down onto the subcontractor, so the sub is bound by the same
      drawings, specifications, schedule requirements, and performance
      standards the GC already agreed to. A flow-down clause is what lets a GC
      say, with real contractual weight, that a sub's work has to comply with
      the full project specifications even though the sub never read every page
      of the prime contract. It does not, by itself, fix a scope gap between
      two subs. What it does is make clear that the full project, as designed,
      is what each sub agreed to help build, which becomes the anchor a project
      manager points back to when reconciling where a specific piece of
      disputed work actually belongs.
    quiz:
      question: "What does a flow-down clause actually do in a subcontract?"
      options:
        - "It resolves any scope gap between two subcontractors once both of their contracts are signed"
        - "It locks in the subcontractor's final price once the design documents are fully complete"
        - "It pulls the relevant obligations from the owner's prime contract down onto the subcontractor"
      answerIndex: 2
      explanation: >
        A flow-down clause binds the subcontractor to the same drawings,
        specifications, and standards the general contractor already agreed to
        with the owner; it anchors obligations, but does not by itself resolve
        a disputed boundary between two subs.
  - content: >
      Scope gaps do not always surface this cleanly or this early. Sometimes a
      subcontractor's price gets locked in before the subcontract itself is
      fully negotiated and signed, because the schedule cannot wait for legal
      review to finish. In that situation a
      [[concept-letter-of-intent|letter of intent]] lets the GC commit to a
      specific subcontractor at an agreed price and authorize early
      mobilization, long-lead procurement, or shop drawing work, while the full
      subcontract with its complete scope exhibits is still being drafted. A
      letter of intent is useful precisely because it unlocks real progress on
      a real deadline. It is also exactly the moment a scope gap is easiest to
      miss, because the document authorizing work to start is deliberately
      lighter on detail than the subcontract that will eventually replace it. A
      project manager who treats a signed letter of intent as the final word on
      scope, instead of as a placeholder still waiting on the detailed
      exhibits, is setting up the exact gap this lesson opened with.
    quiz:
      question: "Why does a letter of intent create real risk for scope gaps, even though it helps the schedule?"
      options:
        - "It requires the full subcontract to be signed before the subcontractor can begin any mobilization or procurement"
        - "It functions as a legally binding replacement for the subcontract once both parties sign it"
        - "It is deliberately lighter on scope detail than the subcontract that will eventually replace it"
      answerIndex: 2
      explanation: >
        A letter of intent authorizes early mobilization or procurement at an
        agreed price while the detailed scope exhibits are still being drafted,
        which makes it an easy moment to mistake a placeholder for a finished
        scope agreement.
  - content: >
      Catching a gap before it becomes a field problem is mostly a matter of
      discipline, not talent. A buyout scope reconciliation is the practice of
      laying every trade's final, signed scope of work side by side against the
      design drawings and the specifications, line by line, looking
      specifically for work that falls outside every sub's scope entirely, or
      worse, inside two subs' scope at once. Done well, this happens before
      mobilization, while a gap is still a spreadsheet problem. Done late, or
      not at all, the same gap gets discovered by a foreman standing in front
      of an unfinished rooftop unit asking who is supposed to run the wire. The
      second version costs real money and real schedule days, because by then
      the fix usually means a change order, a frustrated sub, or both. The
      habit of reconciling scope early is unglamorous. It is also one of the
      clearest differences between a project that runs smoothly and one that
      spends its first month untangling arguments about who owns what.
    quiz:
      question: "What is a buyout scope reconciliation, and when does it do the most good?"
      options:
        - "A final cost audit the owner's accounting team performs after the project has already reached substantial completion"
        - "A negotiation tactic the general contractor leans on mainly after a subcontractor has already filed a formal claim"
        - "Comparing every trade's signed scope against the drawings and specs, done before mobilization while a gap is still a spreadsheet problem"
      answerIndex: 2
      explanation: >
        Reconciling scope line by line before work starts catches gaps while
        they are still cheap to fix; the same gap discovered in the field after
        mobilization usually costs real money and schedule time to resolve.
  - content: >
      A gap that never gets reconciled tends to grow rather than resolve
      itself, and the name for that growth is
      [[concept-scope-creep|scope creep]]. Scope creep usually gets talked
      about as an owner adding requests mid-project, but it shows up just as
      easily at the boundary between two subcontractors, where unclear scope
      slowly expands to cover whatever nobody else claims, without anyone
      formally pricing or approving the change. A sub who quietly absorbs a
      piece of disputed work to keep the schedule moving today is often the
      same sub filing a claim for extra compensation months later, once the
      pattern has repeated often enough to add up. Reconciling scope at buyout,
      flowing the right obligations down through the subcontract, and treating
      a letter of intent as a bridge rather than a finished agreement are all
      ways of keeping that boundary honest from the start. For readers drawn to
      this kind of precision, a
      [[career-contracts-administrator|Contracts Administrator]] spends entire
      days on exactly this work, and the HardHatU
      [[interview-business|Business interview guide]] has more on what that
      career, and the Project Manager role next to it, actually look like day
      to day.
    quiz:
      question: "How does scope creep commonly show up at the boundary between two subcontractors?"
      options:
        - "It becomes much less likely once a flow-down clause ties both subcontracts to the same prime contract language"
        - "It happens mainly when the owner formally requests additional work in writing partway through construction"
        - "Unclear scope slowly expands to cover disputed work without anyone formally pricing or approving the change"
      answerIndex: 2
      explanation: >
        A subcontractor who quietly absorbs disputed work to keep the job
        moving today is often the same subcontractor filing a claim for extra
        compensation later, once the pattern has repeated enough to add up.
