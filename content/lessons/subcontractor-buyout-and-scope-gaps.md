sections:
  - content: >
      Picture yourself as a [[career-project-manager|Project Manager]] a few
      weeks into a renovation job, comparing two subcontractors' scope
      exclusions side by side. Both documents look clean on their own. Read
      together, they reveal a gap: the electrical sub excluded the rooftop
      units' control wiring, assuming mechanical covered it. Mechanical
      excluded the exact same wiring, assuming electrical had it. Nobody lied,
      nobody missed a line item. Two [[concept-scope-of-work|scopes of work]]
      were each internally consistent, and together they still left a real
      piece of the building with nobody responsible for it.
      [[lesson-bidding-and-winning-work|Winning the bid]] answers who gets the
      job. Making sure every piece of that job actually lands on someone's
      price is a separate, quieter job, and it's a core part of running one.
    quiz:
      question: "What made the rooftop-unit control wiring a true scope gap rather than a missed line item?"
      options:
        - "The electrical subcontractor's bid left out the control wiring because of a pricing error during its final review"
        - "Both subcontractors' scopes of work excluded the same piece of work, each reasonably assuming the other covered it"
        - "The project's drawings showed the rooftop units but left their control wiring routing unresolved between the trades"
      answerIndex: 1
      explanation: >
        Each subcontractor's exclusions were internally consistent and
        individually reasonable. The gap existed only because the two scopes,
        read together, left the work with nobody responsible for it.
  - content: >
      The fix for a gap like that starts with the subcontract, not a phone
      call. Most subcontracts carry a
      [[concept-flow-down-clause|flow-down clause]] , language that pulls the
      owner's requirements on the general contractor down onto the
      subcontractor too. It's what lets a GC say, with real contractual
      weight, that a sub's work has to meet the full project specs, even if
      the sub never read every page of the prime contract. A flow-down clause
      doesn't fix a scope gap by itself. What it does is make clear that the
      whole project, as designed, is what every sub agreed to help build, and
      that's the anchor a project manager points back to when sorting out who
      owns a specific piece of disputed work.
    quiz:
      question: "What does a flow-down clause actually do in a subcontract?"
      options:
        - "It pulls the relevant obligations from the owner's prime contract down onto the subcontractor"
        - "It locks in the subcontractor's final price once the design documents are fully complete"
        - "It resolves any scope gap between two subcontractors automatically, once both of their contracts are signed"
      answerIndex: 0
      explanation: >
        A flow-down clause binds the subcontractor to the same drawings and
        standards the GC already agreed to with the owner. It anchors
        obligations, but doesn't by itself resolve a disputed boundary.
  - content: >
      Scope gaps don't always surface this cleanly. Sometimes a price gets
      locked in before the subcontract itself is fully signed, because the
      schedule can't wait for legal review. A
      [[concept-letter-of-intent|letter of intent]] lets the GC commit to a
      sub at an agreed price and authorize early work, like mobilization or
      ordering long-lead materials, while the full contract is still being
      drafted. It's useful because it unlocks real progress on a real
      deadline. It's also the easiest moment to miss a scope gap, because the
      document authorizing work is deliberately lighter on detail than the
      contract that will eventually replace it.
    quiz:
      question: "Why does a letter of intent create real risk for scope gaps, even though it helps the schedule?"
      options:
        - "It requires the full subcontract to be signed before the subcontractor can begin any mobilization or procurement"
        - "It functions as a legally binding replacement for the subcontract once both parties have signed it"
        - "It is deliberately lighter on scope detail than the subcontract that will eventually replace it"
      answerIndex: 2
      explanation: >
        A letter of intent authorizes early mobilization or procurement at an
        agreed price while the detailed scope exhibits are still being
        drafted, which makes it easy to mistake for a finished agreement.
  - content: >
      Catching a gap before it hits the field is mostly discipline, not
      talent. A buyout scope reconciliation means laying every trade's signed
      scope side by side against the drawings and specs, looking specifically
      for work that falls outside every sub's scope, or worse, inside two
      subs' scope at once. Done early, it's a spreadsheet problem. Done late,
      it's a foreman standing in front of an unfinished rooftop unit asking
      who's supposed to run the wire, and by then the fix usually means a
      change order, a frustrated sub, or both.
    quiz:
      question: "What is a buyout scope reconciliation, and when does it do the most good?"
      options:
        - "Comparing every trade's signed scope against the drawings and specs, done before mobilization while a gap is still cheap to fix"
        - "A negotiation tactic the general contractor leans on mainly after a subcontractor has already filed a formal claim"
        - "A final cost audit the owner's accounting team performs after the project has already reached substantial completion"
      answerIndex: 0
      explanation: >
        Reconciling scope line by line before work starts catches gaps while
        they're still cheap to fix. The same gap found in the field after
        mobilization usually costs real money and schedule time.
  - content: >
      A gap that never gets caught tends to grow instead of going away, and
      that's [[concept-scope-creep|scope creep]] . People usually think of
      scope creep as an owner adding requests mid-project, but it shows up
      just as easily at the boundary between two subcontractors, where unclear
      scope slowly expands to cover whatever nobody else claims. A sub who
      quietly absorbs disputed work to keep the schedule moving today is often
      the same sub filing a claim for it months later. Reconciling scope at
      buyout and treating a letter of intent as a bridge, not a finished
      agreement, are both ways of keeping that boundary honest from the start.
      For readers drawn to this kind of precision, a
      [[career-contracts-administrator|Contracts Administrator]] spends entire
      days on exactly this work, and the HardHatU
      [[interview-business|Business interview guide]] has more on what that
      career looks like.
    quiz:
      question: "How does scope creep commonly show up at the boundary between two subcontractors?"
      options:
        - "It happens mainly when the owner formally requests additional work in writing partway through construction"
        - "It becomes much less likely once a flow-down clause ties both subcontracts to the same prime contract language"
        - "Unclear scope slowly expands to cover disputed work without anyone formally pricing or approving the change"
      answerIndex: 2
      explanation: >
        A subcontractor who quietly absorbs disputed work to keep the job
        moving today is often the same one filing a claim for it later, once
        the pattern has repeated enough to add up.
